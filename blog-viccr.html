★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

VicCr

Tech My House

Santa Ana, CA
House / Tech House
KEEPITIL Artist

The Instagram handle says it all: Techmyhousemuzicsf. VicCr is a House and Tech House DJ based in Santa Ana who has taken the technical precision of Tech House and married it to the soulful warmth that classic house was built on. Orange County's underground has embraced this approach wholeheartedly — because in a genre landscape often divided between feeling and function, VicCr refuses to choose. Santa Ana, one of the most culturally rich cities in the county, provides a perfect home base for music that bridges heritage and innovation. When the house is this warm and the tech is this clean, the room doesn't need to pick a side.

"Tech House only works when the house is still in it. I never let that go."
THE STORY
What drew you specifically to House music before you arrived at Tech House?
House music found me before I was old enough to understand what it was. Growing up in Santa Ana, music was everywhere — in the neighborhoods, in the cars, at family gatherings — and some of that music had the rhythmic signature of house even when it wasn't being called house. The connection between house and Latin music, between house and funk and soul, is something I understood culturally before I understood it musically. When I eventually started going to electronic music events and heard proper deep house and classic house, it felt like recognition rather than discovery. This is the music I was already hearing — it just finally had a name.
How did the transition to Tech House happen?
Gradually and then completely. I started noticing that some of the records I was most excited to play had this quality — they had the groove of House but with a tighter, more mechanical texture, a kind of precision that pure house sometimes trades away in favor of warmth. Those were Tech House records, and once I identified the category, I went deep into it. What I found was that Tech House at its best doesn't choose between feeling and function — it earns them both simultaneously. The technical precision becomes a different kind of feeling, the sense that every element is exactly where it's supposed to be. That's an emotion too, even if it's cooler in temperature than classic house.
What does Santa Ana's music community mean to your work?
Santa Ana is one of the most overlooked cities in California for culture, which is remarkable given how rich it actually is. It's a majority-Latino city with deep roots in arts and music, a downtown that has supported creative businesses for decades, and a community that takes culture seriously as a form of identity and resistance. Playing for Santa Ana crowds means playing for people who understand music as something that belongs to them, not something they're spectators of. That sense of ownership, of the crowd being active participants rather than passive recipients, is something I carry into every set regardless of where I'm playing. Santa Ana taught me that.
How does your IG name "Techmyhousemuzicsf" reflect your actual philosophy?
It's a declaration of intent: I'm taking house music and applying a technical lens to it. "Tech my house" is not about replacing the house — it's about enhancing it, sharpening it, giving it tools it didn't have before without stripping it of what made it essential. The "sf" at the end is a nod to the Bay Area house tradition that I've always respected enormously, even as I operate primarily in Southern California. It reflects an aspiration and an alignment — this is music that connects geographically and spiritually to a longer California house tradition, wherever it's currently being made. The name is a whole thesis statement compressed into a handle.
THE CRAFT
What do you see as the real difference between House feeling and Tech House structure?
Classic house is built around human imperfection — the slightly swung rhythm, the live percussion element, the vocal that wasn't perfectly tuned because the feeling mattered more than the pitch. Tech House is built around machine precision — the locked grid, the tight kick, the bass that sits exactly in its frequency range without wandering. Neither is superior. They're different emotional technologies. House says: this is made by people for people, and the humanity is the point. Tech House says: the machine can groove too, and there's a specific joy in hearing something mechanical move with genuine soul. My job is to find the space where both of those things are simultaneously true.
Who are the artists and labels that have most defined your approach?
Fisher showed me that Tech House can be completely unpretentious — that it can be funny, irreverent, unapologetically fun while still being technically excellent. Chris Lake has this quality of making precision feel organic, of building tracks that sound machine-made but groove like they were played by a band. The Dirtybird label is a whole philosophy in itself: take house music seriously enough to keep it weird and playful, never let it become self-important. And Maya Jane Coles represents the depth end of Tech House — sets that are technically immaculate but emotionally layered, that reward close attention without demanding it. Between those references, I have most of my artistic blueprint.
How do you keep the soul in the technical side when you're mixing?
By listening to the music rather than watching the waveforms. The moment you start DJing primarily visually — watching the screen, watching the meters, counting bars by sight rather than by feel — you've started making technical decisions instead of musical ones. I try to build my technical skills to the point where they're automatic, so that when I'm actually playing, my conscious attention is fully on what the music is doing and what the crowd needs, not on the mechanics of the transition. The soul stays in it when the technical execution is so practiced that it disappears, leaving only the music.
What advice would you give to new House DJs about keeping soul in a technical genre?
Learn the history before you learn the tools. If you start with your controller and your DAW before you've listened to a hundred classic house records, you'll build technical skills without a foundation to put them on. The soul in house music is not something you add — it's something you inherit from a lineage, and you can only inherit it by actually listening, deeply and repeatedly, to the people who built the form. Once that's in your ears, it will come out in your mixing automatically, even when you're playing the most technically precise material. The history inoculates you against soullessness.
THE SCENE
What is the OC House and Tech House scene offering right now?
More than it's getting credit for, honestly. There are consistent underground events in Orange County that are booking quality DJs, maintaining good sound systems, and building loyal communities of regulars. The scene isn't as visible as LA's because it's smaller and because OC still has a commercial reputation that overshadows the underground work happening here. But the underground is doing what underground scenes always do when they're healthy: growing slowly, building quality relationships, and producing music and nights that will matter to the people involved for years. The visibility will come. The substance is already here.
How do you think about the commercial side of Tech House vs. the underground?
Tech House has an interesting relationship with the mainstream because it's one of the few underground genres that actually crossed over without entirely losing its soul. There are commercial Tech House acts playing festival main stages, and there are underground Tech House DJs playing warehouse parties for three hundred people, and both can be excellent at what they do. The mistake is assuming one cancels the other out. I don't resent the commercial side — if it brings new ears to the genre, some of those new ears will eventually find the underground. My job is to be here when they do, doing something that shows them what depth looks like.
What does being part of KEEPITIL represent for you personally?
It represents the moment when the community you've been building in acknowledges that what you're doing is real and worth preserving. I've been playing in OC for years without seeking large platforms, focused on the music and the rooms rather than the recognition. KEEPITIL is the kind of platform that finds you when you're doing it right rather than requiring you to perform for attention. Being included here alongside other California underground artists who are equally committed to the work feels like a genuine validation of the approach: keep your head down, keep the music good, and eventually the right people notice. That's the KEEPITIL ethos, and it matches mine exactly.
