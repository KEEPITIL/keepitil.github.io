★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

elysium

Where Bass Becomes Transcendence

Los Angeles, CA
Bass / Dubstep
KEEPITIL Artist

The name says it all. elysium — the place of perfect happiness in Greek mythology — is a Los Angeles Bass and Dubstep artist who believes heavy music can be transcendent music. Where others see bass weight as aggression, elysium finds the emotional core of it: the frequencies that don't just move bodies but bypass the mind entirely and speak directly to something deeper. LA's underground has been listening, and what they've heard is a DJ who treats sub-bass as a spiritual medium rather than a blunt instrument. In a city that has seen every possible version of electronic music come and go, elysium has found the version that feels genuinely new.

"Bass isn't violence. It's intimacy at a cellular level."
THE STORY
How did you find Dubstep beyond its commercial phase?
For most people my age, Dubstep entered the picture during the brostep era — the big American festival version that was everywhere around 2011 and 2012. That wasn't wrong, it just wasn't the whole picture. I started digging backward, into the UK origins, into the South London sound system culture that actually gave birth to all of it, and what I found there was something completely different from what I'd been shown. The early Dubstep records are these slow, cavernous, incredibly emotional things — they're closer to dub reggae in spirit than to festival EDM. That's what got me. Once I heard that, I couldn't unhear it, and everything I've done since has been about holding onto that emotional core.
What does the LA bass music community look like from inside it?
Los Angeles has one of the most serious bass music communities in the country, and a lot of people outside California don't know that. There's a whole ecosystem here — promoters who have been doing this for over a decade, sound systems that are specifically calibrated for sub-bass performance, and a crowd that actually knows the difference between a good bass set and a great one. The community is tight-knit without being closed off, which is a hard balance to strike. I've been welcomed by people who've been in it for years, and I try to pass that forward to anyone coming up now who's serious about the music rather than just the aesthetic.
What is the emotional dimension of Bass music that people miss?
People often talk about bass music in purely physical terms — the drop, the impact, the volume. And those things are real and matter. But the emotional dimension is what separates a transcendent set from a loud one. When bass frequencies are deployed with intention, they trigger something physiological — your chest resonates, your perception of space changes, your sense of where your body ends and the room begins gets blurry. That's not aggression. That's intimacy. The best bass music I've ever heard makes me feel profoundly close to the people around me, as though we're all sharing a single nervous system for the duration of the track. That's what I'm trying to recreate every time I play.
How do you select tracks for maximum impact without burning the crowd out?
Dynamics are everything. If every track hits equally hard, nothing hits hard — your brain recalibrates to whatever the baseline is and stops noticing. So I think about contrast constantly. I'll pull the energy back with something atmospheric and spacious, let the room breathe, build some anticipation, and then bring the weight back in when it can actually be felt. The difference between a DJ who plays heavy and a DJ who plays smart is that the smart one understands rest as a musical element, not just dead time between the big moments.
THE CRAFT
Which artists have shaped your sound and what did you take from each?
Burial is the reason I understand atmosphere as a weapon — those records are as much about what's missing from the sound as what's present, and that sense of negative space has deeply influenced how I build tension in a set. Skream showed me that Dubstep could be genuinely joyful, not just dark — there's a playfulness in his early work that people overlook because they're too busy noticing how heavy it is. Digital Mystikz are the purists, the philosophers of the form, and listening to them taught me that patience in a mix is a form of respect for the audience. And Eptic introduced me to the idea that bass music and melody don't have to be enemies — that a devastatingly heavy track can also be devastatingly beautiful.
How do you build a set that feels like a journey rather than a playlist?
A journey has intention — you know where you're going even if the audience doesn't yet. I always start a set with a rough emotional map: where is this room right now, where do I want it to be in two hours, and what's the path between those two points? The individual track choices are tactical decisions within that larger strategic vision. A playlist is just a sequence of good songs. A journey is a sequence of good songs that are speaking to each other, building on each other, creating a cumulative experience that no single track could achieve on its own. The difference is almost entirely in the DJ's intention before the first record plays.
What technical elements define your mixing approach?
I obsess over low-end management above everything else. Bass music mixes can turn into a muddy mess quickly if you're not disciplined about what's happening in the sub-bass frequencies when two tracks are playing simultaneously. I use EQ very actively — cutting bass on the outgoing track before I bring in the incoming one, managing the overlap carefully so the sub never becomes formless. Beyond that, I try to make my transitions feel inevitable rather than mechanical. The listener should hear the new track arriving and feel like it was always supposed to be next, even if they couldn't have predicted it themselves.
What is the relationship between production and DJing in your practice?
They feed each other constantly. When I'm producing, I'm thinking about how a track will function in a DJ context — how it opens, how it closes, where the energy sits relative to what might come before or after it. When I'm DJing, I notice gaps in my library, things I want to say musically that I don't have a track for, and those gaps become the seeds of new production. I don't think of them as separate disciplines. They're two sides of the same conversation about what bass music can do and where it can go.
THE SCENE
How has LA's underground treated deeper, more underground Dubstep?
Better than you might expect, honestly. LA has a reputation for being a commercial market, and that's partly true — there's a lot of festival culture here and a lot of money chasing trends. But the underground has always existed in parallel to all that, and it's stubborn in the best way. The people who come to underground Dubstep nights in LA are not there by accident. They have found this music specifically and intentionally, which means they're listening deeply, and that makes them one of the best audiences I've ever played for. When a room full of educated listeners responds to what you're doing, that feedback loop pushes you to be better.
What does it mean to you that your artist name references mythology?
The Elysian Fields in Greek mythology are where heroes go after death — a paradise earned through virtue and courage, not just given. I think about that in relation to the music. The transcendence I'm chasing in a set is not easy or automatic. It requires the right room, the right sound system, the right crowd energy, and the right sequence of music coming together in a way that can't be entirely planned. When it happens, it genuinely feels like something outside the everyday. The name is aspirational — a reminder to myself that the goal is not just to play well, but to create something that feels like it exists in a different register of human experience.
What's next for elysium?
More production, more shows, and a deeper exploration of where Bass music and more experimental electronic forms can meet. I'm interested in the outer edges of Dubstep — the places where it starts to feel like ambient music, or like industrial music, or like something that doesn't have a name yet. Los Angeles has the infrastructure to support that kind of experimentation if you can find the right venues and the right promoters, and I think the timing is good. There's an appetite right now for music that is both heavy and intelligent, and I want to be one of the people making it.
