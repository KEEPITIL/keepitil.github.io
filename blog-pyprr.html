★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

PYPRR

No Mercy on the Floor

Los Angeles, CA
Hard Techno / Hardcore
KEEPITIL Artist

PYPRR doesn't play music — they deploy it. Based in Los Angeles, this Hard Techno and Hardcore artist operates in the upper register of BPMs where tracks stop being music and start becoming a physical experience. LA's underground rave rooms have felt it. The energy is industrial, unrelenting, and completely intentional — every build, every drop, every transition engineered to push the crowd to a place they didn't know they needed to go. This is not background music. This is a confrontation, and PYPRR is very good at it.

"Hard Techno isn't aggressive for shock value. It's aggressive because it's honest."
THE STORY
How did you come up in the LA rave scene, and what was the path to Hard Techno?
Los Angeles has such a layered underground that you can spend years going to events and still feel like you haven't seen the whole picture. I started out in the broader electronic music ecosystem — going to any event I could find, absorbing everything from Deep House to more aggressive Techno. The shift toward Hard Techno specifically happened gradually as I started gravitating to the rooms and sets that felt the most physically alive. There's something about that industrial intensity that felt more honest to me than a lot of other electronic music I was hearing at the time. LA's underground accommodates extremes really well if you find the right communities.
Which artists were the revelation for you when it came to Hard Techno and Hardcore?
Sara Landry was a huge one — she showed me that Hard Techno could be both technically sophisticated and emotionally overwhelming at the same time, which is a really rare combination. KAS:ST's production approach completely changed how I think about tension and atmosphere in a hard track. Paula Temple brought an experimental, almost confrontational quality that pushed the genre somewhere genuinely avant-garde. And Alignment showed me how Hard Techno could have both the physicality I wanted and a real sense of musical intention behind every choice. These artists weren't just making fast music — they were building worlds.
How do you think about the balance between production and DJing in your practice?
They feed each other in ways that are hard to separate. When I'm producing, I'm always thinking about how a track will function in a live set — where does the energy peak, what kind of transition does it want on either side, how does it interact with the frequencies of the tracks around it? And when I'm DJing, I'm constantly learning things about structure and pacing that inform how I build tracks in the studio. The live set is also where you find out whether your production instincts are actually translating to a physical space and a live audience, which is the only test that ultimately matters.
What does Hard Techno mean to you on an emotional level — beyond the BPMs and the intensity?
Hard Techno for me is about a kind of honesty that's rare in music. A lot of electronic music smooths things over, makes them comfortable and pleasant, and there's nothing wrong with that — but Hard Techno refuses to do that. It puts the discomfort and the tension and the rawness right at the surface, and I think for a lot of people in the underground that's exactly what they're looking for. Dancing to something that intense is almost cathartic — you surrender to it completely or you don't, and that total surrender is what makes it meaningful rather than just aggressive.
THE CRAFT
How does the energy of a warehouse compare to a club when you're playing Hard Techno?
Warehouses are built for this genre in a way that clubs often aren't. The concrete floors, the open ceilings, the raw acoustics — Hard Techno needs room to breathe and reverberate to reach its full impact. When you're playing a 170+ BPM Hardcore track through a proper sound system in a warehouse and you can feel the bass frequencies moving through the floor into your legs, that's the full experience. Club environments can be excellent too, but the industrial aesthetic of a warehouse aligns with the music in a way that enhances both the sonic and emotional impact of what you're playing.
Walk us through how you build a set — how do you sequence tracks across a Hard Techno and Hardcore session?
The opening of a set is all about establishing the world you're building — I usually start slightly below where I want to peak, building atmosphere and industrial texture before the full weight comes in. Hard Techno has incredible range within its intensity level; you can go from tracks that are atmospheric and ominous to tracks that are pure aggression, and that movement is where the storytelling happens. I think about my sets in movements rather than individual tracks — each 20-minute section has an arc, a dynamic shape, and the Hardcore moments are strategic peaks rather than just the default state. A set that's at maximum intensity for the entire duration is exhausting in the wrong way.
What does the future of LA's Hard Techno scene look like to you?
LA's Hard Techno scene is at a genuinely exciting inflection point right now. The genre has been building a dedicated following for several years and the event infrastructure has started to catch up — more promoters are willing to book all-Hard-Techno nights, more venues are accommodating the specific sound system requirements, and the audience is increasingly knowledgeable and committed rather than casual. I think we're about to see a wave of LA-based Hard Techno artists start getting international recognition, which is going to bring more resources and more serious attention to the scene here. It's not just a trend. It has real foundations.
THE SCENE
What makes the LA underground distinctive compared to other cities with strong Techno scenes?
LA has a kind of creative cross-pollination that's unique — because the city has so many different subcultures all running simultaneously, you get Hard Techno DJs who are also deeply influenced by the city's hip hop heritage, or warehouse rave organizers who come from the art world, or producers whose aesthetic sensibility is shaped by the film industry. That collision of influences produces a sound and scene that's harder to pin down than somewhere like Berlin, but it also means that LA underground scenes tend to evolve in more unexpected directions. There's less orthodoxy about what things are supposed to sound like, which can be liberating.
What's your advice to new DJs wanting to break into the Hard Techno world?
Study the genre deeply before you start performing it. Hard Techno has a real history and a real set of values that audiences in this world take seriously, so understanding the lineage — from industrial music through EBM through early Techno into what Hard Techno is now — will make you a better artist and more credible to the community. Also, invest in your mixing technique specifically at high BPMs, because the technical demands of Hard Techno are significant and there's no hiding mistakes when everything is moving that fast. And go to the shows, support the scene, be present before you expect the scene to be present for you.
