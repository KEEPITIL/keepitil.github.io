★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

XEODIN

San Diego's Bass Weight Carrier

San Diego, CA
Dubstep / Tearout / Riddim
KEEPITIL Artist

San Diego has always been an underrated bass music city, and XEODIN is part of why. Operating at the intersection of Dubstep, Tearout, and Riddim — three genres that demand physical commitment from both artist and crowd — XEODIN builds sets that move bodies with the precision of someone who understands exactly where the low end needs to hit. The bass isn't just an element of the music; it's the architecture that everything else hangs from. San Diego's underground has found in XEODIN a DJ who treats the science of the drop with the seriousness it deserves and the instinct of an artist who was born into this sound.

"Riddim is a conversation. Tearout is a statement. I play both."
THE STORY
How did you come up in the San Diego bass music scene?
San Diego has a legitimate underground bass music scene that gets overlooked because LA is right up the freeway and takes most of the attention. But there have always been crews and promoters here who are serious about Dubstep and heavier electronic music, putting on events in warehouses, smaller clubs, and outdoor spaces that give the local community somewhere to go without having to drive two hours north. I grew up going to those events and absorbing the culture before I started playing — really understanding what it means to be in a room when bass music is being played properly through a system that can actually reproduce those frequencies. That physical education in what bass does to a room shaped everything about how I DJ.
Who were the artists that defined your approach to Dubstep and bass music?
Space Laces was a big early influence because the production quality is just on another level — the sound design is so precise that you study it automatically as a bass music practitioner. Subtronics changed how I think about the relationship between Riddim and more melodic Dubstep, showing that you can have both the grid-locked groove of Riddim and genuine emotional content in the same track. Snails brought this almost cartoonishly aggressive energy that was pure fun without sacrificing heaviness, and understanding that playfulness can coexist with intensity was important. Zeds Dead showed me how to have a career in bass music while keeping genuine musical breadth — they span so many subgenres while maintaining a coherent artistic identity.
What does San Diego specifically bring to bass music culture that's different from LA or the rest of California?
San Diego's bass music community has a particular warmth and tightness that comes from being a slightly smaller, slightly more isolated scene. When you're not in the direct shadow of LA's massive event calendar, your local scene has to stand on its own, which tends to build real loyalty between artists, promoters, and audiences. There's also a border culture influence here — being a city right next to Tijuana means that the cultural influences and the sonic palette are different in ways that aren't always obvious but definitely filter into what artists here produce and play. San Diego bass music has its own flavor and I think it's starting to get recognition for that distinctiveness.
THE CRAFT
How do you distinguish between Dubstep, Tearout, and Riddim in your sets — do they have different roles?
Each genre functions differently in the set architecture. Riddim is the groove foundation — it's hypnotic, locked into a pattern, and incredibly effective at building a sustained energy that the crowd can sink into for an extended period. Tearout is when you want maximum aggression and impact, the tracks that physically assault the room and create those peak moments of collective intensity. Dubstep in the broader sense can do both — it can be melodic and emotional, building narrative and taking the crowd somewhere before the drop releases the tension. I use them at different moments in a set based on what the room needs, and the transitions between them are where the skill of reading a crowd really comes into play.
Talk to us about your production work — are you making original music alongside DJing?
Production is a constant background process for me — it's where I work out the ideas about sound design and structure that then inform how I DJ. Bass music production specifically requires a deep technical understanding of how low frequencies behave, how to design basslines that hit hard in a club environment rather than just sounding big on headphones, and how to structure a drop in a way that actually delivers on the tension built in the intro. I've released some tracks and I'm continuously working on more, with the goal of having a body of original work that represents the Dubstep/Tearout/Riddim intersection I'm exploring in my sets. The production process makes me a better DJ because I understand the choices behind every element of every track I play.
What makes Southern California unique as a place to develop a bass music identity?
Southern California has one of the most diverse and technically demanding bass music audiences in the world. People here have heard a lot, they've been to a lot of shows, and they can tell immediately whether you're bringing something real or just going through the motions. That audience quality forces you to level up constantly — there's no coasting on a familiar sound if the crowd has heard every iteration of that sound already. The geography also helps because you have LA, San Diego, the IE, and OC all within a couple of hours of each other, which means you can play different types of rooms and different types of crowds regularly, which develops your versatility in ways that playing one city exclusively cannot.
THE SCENE
What's happening in the San Diego underground right now that people should know about?
San Diego's underground bass scene is in a genuinely healthy place right now. There are committed promoters putting on regular events with real production values, a growing roster of local artists who are developing at a high level, and an audience that's increasingly passionate and knowledgeable. The city is large enough to sustain a real underground economy around music but small enough that the community is tight and supportive rather than anonymous and competitive. I think we're approaching a moment where San Diego bass music starts getting recognized more broadly as having its own identity and its own important artists, rather than being treated as LA's smaller neighbor.
What's your advice to new bass music producers in San Diego who want to get their music heard?
Play in public as soon as you have something that sounds finished, even if it's just at a friend's house or a small open-format night. The gap between how music sounds in your headphones during production and how it sounds on a system in a real room is massive, and you need to close that gap through experience rather than just theory. Also, build relationships with other local artists rather than viewing them as competition — the San Diego bass scene is better served by a collaborative attitude than an individualistic one, and the connections you make with other artists in your city will open more doors than self-promotion ever will. Go to shows, be part of the community, and the community will be part of you.
Where does XEODIN go from here?
The immediate goal is more original releases and more shows across Southern California and beyond. I want to build a catalog of tracks that represent the Dubstep/Tearout/Riddim combination in a way that's distinctly mine — not just playing in the style of my influences but pushing those genres into a space that reflects my own artistic perspective. I'm also deeply committed to the San Diego scene specifically and want to keep investing in it — playing local shows, working with local promoters, supporting other San Diego artists. The underground grows when the people inside it commit to building it. That's something I take seriously as part of what XEODIN means.
