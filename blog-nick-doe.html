★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

Nick Doe

The Inland Empire's Open Secret

Highland, CA
Underground Electronic
KEEPITIL Artist

The Inland Empire doesn't get the nightlife press of LA or the cultural mystique of San Francisco. Nick Doe is fine with that. Based in Highland, deep in San Bernardino County, Nick Doe has been putting in the work on a regional underground scene that operates largely beneath the radar of California's bigger music markets. The Inland Empire stretches across a vast swath of Southern California that most coastal residents drive through without stopping, but those who live and create here know there's a rich, underserved community of music lovers hungry for the kind of experience that the underground provides. Nick Doe found that community and started feeding it — building connections across the IE that link together people who had been going to events in isolation without knowing there was a whole network of underground culture happening around them. The sets are real. The community is real. The Inland Empire underground is real — and Nick Doe is one of its most visible and committed proof points.

"The IE underground doesn't perform authenticity. We live it."

THE STORY
What is the Highland and IE underground scene actually like — paint us a picture?
Highland itself is a small city that most people don't have a clear image of — it's between San Bernardino and Redlands, nestled against the foothills, and it has a lot of the character of the broader IE: working class, diverse, and not interested in performing for an outside audience. The underground scene across the IE as a whole is spread out across a big geographic area, so events happen in a lot of different cities — San Bernardino, Riverside, Fontana, Ontario — and the community that shows up to them comes from all over the region. What ties it together is a shared understanding that this music and this culture isn't happening for anyone's benefit except the people who genuinely love it. That creates an atmosphere at IE underground events that's distinct from what you find in more visible markets.
How does the IE compare to LA when it comes to underground music culture?
LA's underground is incredible and I have deep respect for what's happening there, but it operates under a weight of visibility that changes things. In LA, even the underground is observed — there are journalists, tastemakers, and industry people at events, and the knowledge that those people might be watching creates a subtle performance dynamic that you can't fully eliminate. In the IE, that outside gaze is almost entirely absent, which means the underground here is purely for itself. The flip side is that you have to work harder to find community and to get information about what's happening, because there's less infrastructure and less documentation. That's exactly the gap KEEPITIL is starting to fill, and it matters enormously for making the IE scene legible to itself.
What drives you to DJ in a region without the major venues or industry infrastructure?
The absence of that infrastructure is actually a big part of the appeal. When you're not working within a system that already has grooves worn into it, you get to make the decisions about what a great event looks like rather than fitting into someone else's template. Playing in the IE means you're building something rather than plugging into something that already exists, and there's a satisfaction in that kind of construction that I don't think you get in more established scenes. The people who show up to underground events in the IE showed up because they wanted to be there, not because a promoter with a big budget spent money getting them there — and that self-selection creates a crowd energy that more than compensates for the logistical challenges of operating in a less developed market.
How did you find your community in the IE underground?
It was a slow accumulation of connections over time — going to events, talking to the same people repeatedly, eventually starting to DJ at small events and building outward from there. The IE underground community is tight enough that once you're in, you're in — people are genuinely supportive of each other in a way that I think reflects the shared experience of operating without much external validation. Social media has been important for connecting the dots between people in different IE cities who are doing similar things but might not have ever crossed paths in person. The combination of in-person event presence and digital connection is what makes the IE scene feel like a scene rather than just a collection of isolated events happening in the same general region.

THE CRAFT
How would you describe your musical style — what does a Nick Doe set sound like?
My sets tend toward the heavier end of underground electronic — music that has real weight and momentum to it, that fills a physical space and creates a bodily experience rather than just a listening one. The IE audience that I know best responds to that kind of physical intensity, and it reflects my own taste as a listener. Within that heavy framework, though, I'm interested in texture and variety — I'm not looking to blast people with the same energy for two hours straight, because that becomes numbing rather than moving. The best sets I've played have had moments of genuine release and relief embedded in the intensity, which makes the heavy parts hit harder by contrast.
What musical influences shaped your approach to DJing and sound selection?
I came up listening to a mix of underground electronic music and hip-hop, and both of those traditions inform how I think about rhythm and energy in a set. The underground electronic world taught me about extended energy arcs and the importance of physical sound design — how the frequency content of music affects how people feel it in their bodies. Hip-hop gave me an education in how beats work at a granular level and how much can be communicated rhythmically before a single note of melody is involved. Those two influences combine in my sets in ways that I think give them a distinctive character, even when the music I'm playing is squarely in the electronic genre.
How do you approach music discovery and building out your library?
I spend a lot of time in the channels where underground music actually lives — Bandcamp, SoundCloud, Beatport, and the networks of recommendations that come through trusted contacts in the scene. The best music discoveries usually come through people rather than through algorithmic recommendations, because the humans in your network are selecting for the same values you have rather than optimizing for engagement metrics. I keep organized collections that allow me to move quickly in a live set when I need to, but I also keep a separate category of things that are new or uncertain, music I'm still figuring out how to use — and sometimes those experimental choices in a set create the most memorable moments.
What are your future plans for building your presence in the IE scene?
The immediate goal is to keep deepening the connections across the IE — building relationships with promoters, venues, and artists in different parts of the region so that the underground community here starts to feel more cohesive. Beyond that, I want to start bringing artists from outside the IE into the region more regularly, because I think cross-pollination with what's happening in OC, LA, and the broader California underground would benefit everyone. The IE underground has something real, and exposing it to more outside influence while maintaining what makes it distinctive is the balancing act I'm thinking about. The longer-term vision is for the IE to be recognized as a legitimate underground music region, not just a suburb of LA.
THE SCENE
What would surprise people about the IE underground who only know it from the outside?
People who know the IE mostly through the commuter-belt stereotype would be surprised by the depth of creative culture that exists here. The region has a large population with genuine cultural hunger, and when you give that population a quality underground music experience, the response is intense and committed in ways that you don't always see in scenes with more options available. The IE audience is not jaded — they haven't been overserved by an abundance of underground events, so when something good happens, it lands with full force. That makes performing here a uniquely rewarding experience, because the room gives you everything they have in return for giving them something real.
What role does San Bernardino County play in California's underground music landscape?
San Bernardino County is one of the largest counties by area in the entire country, which means the underground scene here is necessarily decentralized and community-by-community in a way that differs from denser regions. That decentralization actually creates resilience — there's no single scene hub that, if it disappeared, would take everything with it. The underground culture across San Bernardino County exists in pockets that connect through networks of people and events, and that connective tissue is what makes it a real scene rather than just a series of isolated happenings. The challenge is making those connections visible and intentional rather than leaving them to chance encounters.
What's the message you want the California underground community to take away from the IE scene?
That authenticity isn't a coastal exclusive. The things that make underground culture worth caring about — genuine community, honest music, real experience — exist in the IE with as much intensity and integrity as anywhere in California. The IE underground doesn't have the press coverage or the influencer presence, but it has the thing that those things are supposed to represent: a community of people who genuinely care about music and culture and who show up for each other consistently over time. That's what matters, and the IE has it. We just need the rest of California to know it's here.
