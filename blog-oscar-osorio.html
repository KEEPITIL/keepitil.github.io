★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

OSCAR OSORIO

Deep Roots in the OC Underground

Santa Ana, CA
Underground House / Deep House
KEEPITIL Artist

Deep House is a slow burn. Oscar Osorio knows this, and he uses it deliberately. Based in Santa Ana, Orange County, Oscar Osorio has been building a reputation in the OC underground for sets that value atmosphere over spectacle, groove over hype, and the long arc of an evening over individual moments of impact. Chicago's house music heritage lives in these sets. OC's late-night underground has given them a home.

"Deep House doesn't rush. The right groove will get there."
THE STORY
How did you get into Deep House specifically — what was the moment it clicked?
It was a late-night set at a small venue in the OC where someone played a run of Larry Heard records back-to-back — "Can You Feel It," "Mystery of Love," "Washing Machine" — and I understood for the first time what a groove could do to a room when it was given enough time. It wasn't exciting in the way that music I'd been exposed to before was exciting — it was something deeper than excitement, more like the feeling of a space opening up inside your chest. That's the Larry Heard quality, the quality that made him "Mr. Fingers" — the sense that the music is feeling something, that the synthesizer and the drum machine are expressing genuine emotion. I went home and started digging immediately and I haven't stopped.
Talk about the Chicago house influences that are most present in your sets.
Larry Heard is foundational and unavoidable — his understanding of space, of the relationship between bass and melody, of the way a chord played on a synthesizer can carry the full weight of human longing is something I return to constantly both as a reference and as an active part of my sets. Robert Owens, who sang on many of Heard's most important records, showed me that vocals in house music are not decorations but confessions — the soul element in Deep House is literal, not metaphorical. Ron Trent for the way his productions draw the Chicago tradition forward while incorporating influences from jazz and gospel that give the music extraordinary harmonic depth. These artists built a vocabulary that is still being explored forty years later.
What's the difference between your approach to Underground House and Deep House — are they really different things?
For me they're points on a continuum rather than separate genres. Deep House is the aesthetic — the chord structures, the soulful vocal samples or live vocals, the relatively slower tempo and emphasis on atmosphere over impact. Underground House is a disposition — a resistance to the commercial smoothing-out of house music, a commitment to the original spirit and community values of the form regardless of which specific sonic territory you're working in. A set can be Underground House without being Deep specifically, and Deep House can be done in a commercial way that doesn't honor the underground tradition. I try to do both: the deepness in the sound, the underground in the spirit.
What does Santa Ana's cultural mix bring to your musical identity?
Santa Ana is one of the most authentically Latino cities in California — a city with deep Mexican and Central American roots, with a cultural richness that mainstream Orange County consistently undervalues. Growing up in that community, surrounded by music that values emotional directness, collective celebration, the function of dance as community building rather than individual entertainment, gave me a foundation for understanding what house music is actually for. House music in Chicago was also music made by communities that mainstream America was ignoring — Black and Latino queer communities on the South Side. There's a direct line between the spirit of those original Chicago dance floors and the spirit of the Santa Ana communities I grew up in, and that connection informs everything I do as a DJ.
THE CRAFT
Why is patience a skill in DJing — what does rushing cost you?
Rushing tells the room that you don't trust the music, and if you don't trust the music, the room won't either. A great Deep House track needs time to establish itself, to let the groove settle into people's bodies before they're consciously aware of responding to it — if you mix out after two minutes because you're anxious about the energy level, you've deprived both the track and the crowd of the thing that makes Deep House work. Patience also gives you information: a room that's allowed to fully inhabit a groove will tell you exactly what it needs next, but a room that's been rushed from track to track is too disoriented to communicate anything useful. The patient DJ is also the better-informed DJ.
How do you read a room over the course of a long set — what are you watching for?
Bodies first: where are people positioned on the floor, are they facing the booth or facing each other, are they moving from the hips or from the shoulders. Hip movement tells you the groove is working; shoulder movement tells you the energy is rising but hasn't settled into the body yet. I'm also listening to the room's ambient noise — conversations that go quiet when a certain track drops, the sound of feet on the floor, the physical sound of people being in a space together. Late in a long set I'm particularly attentive to the relationship between the most committed dancers and the people at the edge of the floor — when the edge people start moving toward the center, you know you've built something real. That's the signal to stay exactly where you are and let the music finish what it started.
What's your approach to building a record collection for Deep House — what are you hunting?
I dig in the original Chicago catalogs obsessively — Trax Records, DJ International, early Def Mix releases — because there's material there that the current generation of Deep House DJs isn't playing, music that sounds completely contemporary because it was so far ahead of its time when it was made. For new music I'm following Delusions of Grandeur in the UK, Music For Dreams in Denmark, and a handful of smaller operations that understand that real Deep House requires a certain patience in production as well as in DJing. I'm also increasingly drawn to music from outside the traditional house lineage — Brazilian soul, Japanese city pop, certain strains of ambient electronic — that shares the emotional DNA of Deep House even without the four-four kick.
What makes a Deep House set fail — what are the common mistakes?
Playing too many records in a given hour is the most common error — it's a sign of anxiety rather than confidence, and it prevents any track from doing its full work. Related to this is mixing too early: pulling out of a track before the groove has fully established itself in the room is a form of interruption that resets the emotional state rather than building on it. The other failure mode is tonal inconsistency — moving from genuinely deep, spiritually connected material to something that's merely smooth without recognizing that "smooth" and "deep" are not the same quality. Deep House has emotional weight. Smooth house has production quality without necessarily having soul. Knowing the difference in a record collection and then communicating that difference in a set is the whole craft.
THE SCENE
What is Orange County's underground House music community like right now?
OC's underground is small but serious — people who are genuinely committed to house music as a culture and a practice rather than as a lifestyle accessory. Santa Ana has been a consistent hub for underground events, partly because of the density of the Latino community that has always been central to house music's Southern California story, and partly because the city has spaces and venues that are accessible in a way that LA's real estate situation has made increasingly difficult. There's a strong connection between the OC underground and the LA underground, with artists and attendees moving between scenes, which gives both communities more breadth than they'd have in isolation. KEEPITIL is an important part of making those connections visible and permanent.
What does a perfect late-night Deep House set look like for you as the DJ?
Three in the morning, a small room, a sound system that doesn't need to be loud to be physical — one where you feel the bass in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. A crowd that's been dancing for hours and has reached the state where the body moves without the mind's permission, where people's faces have that particular expression that isn't happiness exactly but is better than happiness — a kind of absorbed presence that looks almost like grief from the outside. You play a Larry Heard record and you can feel the room recognize it before they consciously identify it, the way a smell from childhood hits before you know what memory it's activating. That's the perfect late-night Deep House moment. Everything in a set is building toward the possibility of that moment happening.
What do you want people to walk away knowing about Deep House that they might not have understood before?
That it's music with a history and a community and a political dimension that most people who dance to it have never been told about. Deep House came from the Black and Latino queer communities of Chicago's South Side at a moment when those communities were experiencing the AIDS crisis, systematic police violence, economic abandonment, and cultural invisibility. The joy in the music is not innocent of that context — it's the most sophisticated possible response to it, a decision to build beauty and community and ecstatic collective experience in the face of forces that were trying to erase you. Understanding that history doesn't make the music heavier. It makes it more meaningful, more earned, more necessary.
