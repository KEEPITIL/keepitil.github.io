Chlophonic

Bass That Hits Different in the OC Underground

Santa Ana, CA
Bass / Dubstep / House / DnB
KEEPITIL Artist

Chlophonic emerged from Santa Ana's underground electronic scene with a sound that refuses to pick a lane. Equal parts Dubstep weight, House groove, DnB velocity, and raw Bass pressure — sets that keep crowds guessing and dancing from the first drop to the last. Orange County's underground circuit has taken notice, and for good reason: in a scene where genre loyalty is expected, Chlophonic's genre fluidity is the differentiator that makes every set feel like the first time you heard bass music done right.

"I don't play one genre. I play whatever hits hardest in the moment."

THE STORY
How did you get your start in the Orange County underground?
Santa Ana has this really raw energy to it — it's a working-class city with a massive creative underground that doesn't get enough credit. I started going to warehouse events in the IE and OC when I was still figuring out what kind of music I wanted to make. You'd walk into these spaces and hear everything from dubstep to drum and bass in the same night, and something about that total disregard for genre barriers just clicked with me immediately. Orange County's underground isn't the flashiest scene, but it's deeply committed to the music itself, and that environment shaped everything about how I approach a set.
What were the records and artists that first pulled you toward bass music?
Skrillex was a gateway for a lot of people my age, and I'm not embarrassed to say that was true for me too — when "Scary Monsters and Nice Sprites" dropped, it rearranged something in my brain about what electronic music could be. From there I found Chase & Status, which opened the door to DnB proper, and Andy C became a major reference point for understanding how to sequence a set and build energy intelligently. Subtronics came later and showed me how you could make Riddim and Dubstep feel almost jazz-like in their improvisation. Each of those artists gave me a different piece of the puzzle.
Was there a specific moment when you decided to commit to multi-genre DJing rather than specializing?
There was a night at a small warehouse in Garden Grove where I was playing a set and had to read the room completely differently than I'd planned. I'd been intending to do a heavy dubstep set, but the energy in the room was calling for something more groovy, more House-forward. So I shifted, and the crowd responded, and then I pushed it back toward DnB at the peak of the night and they went insane. That night taught me that the genre is secondary to the emotion — you're chasing a feeling, not a BPM range. After that I stopped thinking of myself as any one type of DJ and started thinking of myself as someone who works with all the tools in the box.
What's it like playing small warehouses versus bigger events in OC?
The small warehouse shows are where the real connection happens. When you're in a 150-person room and you can physically see every face, you learn to read an audience in a way that no large festival stage can teach you. The feedback loop is immediate and unforgiving — if you make a bad transition, you feel it in the energy of the room within seconds. Bigger events have their own energy that's incredible, but the warehouses are where I developed my instincts. The OC underground scene is built on those intimate spaces, and I think that's actually one of its great strengths compared to larger markets.

THE CRAFT
How do you actually blend Bass, Dubstep, House, and DnB without the set feeling chaotic?
The key is understanding the emotional function of each genre rather than just its technical characteristics. Bass music in general is about physical impact, but Dubstep creates impact through tension and release, House creates it through groove and movement, DnB through velocity and urgency. When I'm transitioning between them, I'm always thinking about which emotional state I'm moving the crowd from and toward. A half-time Dubstep track can flow into a House groove surprisingly naturally if you find the right key and BPM relationship. I spend a lot of time in the studio not just making music but studying transitions — what works, what doesn't, and why.
What does your production setup look like? Are you making original tracks alongside DJing?
Yes, production is a huge part of it for me — you can't truly understand how to DJ bass music without understanding how it's built. My DAW of choice is Ableton and I use a lot of hardware synthesis for the low-end design because I want those bass tones to feel physical, not just loud. I'm constantly making edits and bootlegs of tracks to make them sit better in my specific style of sets. Having that production background changes how you hear music when you're playing live — you understand why certain combinations work acoustically and energetically, and you can make faster decisions in the moment.
What's your approach to DnB specifically, given how different it is technically from House and Dubstep?
DnB is the genre that demands the most technical precision in a set because the BPMs are so high that any slip in timing is immediately audible. Andy C and Chase & Status taught me that DnB at its best isn't just fast — it has layers of rhythmic complexity that reward close listening even in a loud club environment. When I bring DnB into a set that's been running through House or Dubstep, I typically use it as a peak energy moment, a gear shift that tells the crowd we're going somewhere new. That velocity shift from 140 BPM Dubstep to 174 BPM DnB, when executed right, is one of the most physically exciting moments you can create in an electronic music set.
THE SCENE
What's the state of the OC underground right now, and who should people be paying attention to?
Orange County's underground is genuinely thriving right now, and I think it's partly because LA has gotten so expensive and saturated that a lot of artists and event organizers are looking at OC with fresh eyes. Santa Ana in particular has a strong creative community — it's a city with a lot of cultural energy and a lot of people who are serious about music as more than entertainment. There are promoters and crews doing incredible work to build consistent event infrastructure, which is what any scene needs to sustain itself. The foundations are strong.
What advice would you give to new producers trying to break into the underground electronic scene?
Play every show you can get, regardless of how small or unpaid. The experience of reading a live room is irreplaceable and cannot be taught — it can only be accumulated through hours of actually doing it. Also, stop worrying about genre identity so early in your development. The artists who have the most interesting voices are usually the ones who spent years absorbing widely before narrowing down. And go to other people's shows — not just to network, but to genuinely listen and learn from what works and what doesn't when someone else is behind the decks.
What does the future look like for Chlophonic?
More music, more shows, and more intentionality around the kind of spaces I'm performing in. I want to keep growing in the underground circuit across Southern California — OC, LA, the IE, San Diego — while also starting to put out original tracks that represent the genre-fluid approach I take to DJing. I think there's an audience out there that's hungry for music that doesn't stay in one box, and I want to be part of building that community. KEEPITIL has been an important part of that mission, and I'm excited about what the network is building collectively.
