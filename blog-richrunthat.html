★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

Richrunthat

Anaheim's Underground Is Running

Anaheim, CA
Underground Electronic
KEEPITIL Artist

Anaheim is Disneyland's city. But behind the theme park tourism and the Angels Stadium crowd, there's a real underground electronic music scene operating in the city's back channels, house venues, and off-the-map warehouse spaces — and Richrunthat is one of its architects. Based in OC's largest city, Richrunthat has been building a reputation through consistent energy, genuine community focus, and sets that remind Anaheim's underground that the real magic happens after midnight and well off the main strip. This isn't music for visitors. It's music for locals who know the city better than any tourist map ever could, and who find their connection to each other in the shared space of a well-crafted underground set. Richrunthat represents a strain of Orange County underground culture that refuses to apologize for existing outside of the spotlight — and in that refusal, they've built something that matters.

"Anaheim's underground doesn't need your permission to exist."
THE STORY
What was it like growing up in Anaheim and discovering underground music?
Growing up in Anaheim, you're surrounded by this weird tension between the manufactured magic of the theme parks and the very real, very unglamorous neighborhoods that most visitors never see. Underground music found me through the people I knew — older kids who were going to parties in warehouses and backyard setups where the energy was completely different from anything you'd hear on the radio or at a regular club. The contrast was the thing that got me: here's Anaheim, which the whole world thinks they know, and then here's this completely separate world operating in the same city that nobody outside of it even knows exists. That gap between the visible city and the real city is basically what underground music is.
What does "underground" mean to you specifically — how do you define it?
Underground means the music exists because the people who make it and listen to it need it to exist, not because a label decided it was marketable or a playlist algorithm picked it up. It's a community that self-selects based on genuine interest rather than trend-following. For me personally, underground also means a certain level of honesty in the music — you're not playing to impress people who don't care, you're playing for people who showed up because this is actually what they want to hear. The underground in Anaheim specifically is small enough that you know the regulars, and that intimacy changes everything about how you approach a set.
How have you gone about building community in OC's scene?
It's mostly just showing up consistently and treating every event like it matters, because to the people there, it does. You build community in this scene by being reliable — people need to know that when you're on the lineup, the energy is going to be there, and when you're not on the lineup, you're still in the room supporting the people who are. OC's underground is geographically spread across a lot of cities, so a big part of community-building here is connecting artists and listeners from Anaheim, Orange, Fullerton, Santa Ana, and everywhere else into something that feels like a shared scene rather than a bunch of isolated local pockets. That takes intentional effort over time.
How does Anaheim compare to other OC cities when it comes to underground music culture?
Anaheim is the biggest city in OC by population, which means there's more raw material to work with — more people, more neighborhoods, more spaces where things can happen under the radar. But that size also means the scene can feel more dispersed than in smaller cities where everything gravitates toward one or two spots. What Anaheim has that some other OC cities don't is a density of actual working-class neighborhoods where underground culture has always had a natural home — people who work hard during the week and want something real and intense on the weekend, not a polished club experience designed for Instagram. That's the audience that built Anaheim's underground, and it's what keeps it honest.
THE CRAFT
How would you describe your DJ style and how you approach energy in a set?
My approach is always about building — I'm not trying to drop the hardest thing I have in the first five minutes. I want to take people somewhere, which means starting somewhere and moving through different phases of intensity and texture before you get to the peak. The energy in my sets tends to run high overall, but it's not just sustained volume — it's about finding the moments where you can pull back just enough that when the next hit comes, it lands harder. Anaheim crowds tend to respond to that kind of intentional architecture in a set; they're experienced enough to appreciate the journey rather than just demanding the destination immediately.
What gear and software do you work with, and how has your technical approach evolved?
I've moved through different setups over time, but right now I'm focused on having something that gives me flexibility in live settings where the sound system and the room can be unpredictable. The technical side of DJing is important to get right, but I try not to let gear obsession get in the way of actually focusing on the music and the room. My biggest technical evolution has been learning to read a room in real time and adjust — knowing when the set I planned needs to pivot because the actual crowd energy is pointing somewhere different. That's a skill you only develop through doing a lot of sets in a lot of different environments.
How do you select and organize music for your sets?
I listen to a lot of music constantly and organize things in ways that let me move quickly between tracks when I need to. The crate-digging part of DJing is something I take seriously because I think a good set is built on good source material — the mixing skill matters, but it can't rescue weak music choices. I look for tracks that have something genuine in them, something that feels like it was made because someone needed to make it rather than because someone calculated what the market wanted. That quality translates to a live room in a way that audiences feel even when they can't articulate why.
Where do you see your sound heading — what directions are you exploring?
I want to keep pushing the energy envelope while finding more nuance in how I get there. There's a version of high-energy underground music that's just relentless without any breathing room, and that can be exhausting in the wrong way — I'm interested in finding intensity that has shape and dimension to it. I'm also thinking more about the extended set format, where you have the time and space to really take an audience through multiple phases rather than just delivering one sustained mood. The longer the set, the more it starts to feel like you're actually composing something in real time, and that's the direction I want to develop.
THE SCENE
Where do you see Anaheim's underground heading over the next few years?
The underground in Anaheim has always been resilient because it's not dependent on venue infrastructure that can disappear — it adapts to whatever spaces are available and keeps moving. I think the next few years will see more connection between OC's different underground pockets, with Anaheim artists collaborating more with people from other parts of the county and the broader Southern California underground. The scene is small enough right now that growth feels possible in meaningful ways — not mainstream growth, but the kind of depth and cohesion that makes a scene genuinely strong rather than just momentarily buzzy.
What does KEEPITIL mean for OC's underground community?
KEEPITIL is giving OC's underground a platform that keeps the culture intact rather than packaging it for mass consumption. What matters about having a dedicated platform is that it gives the community a reference point — somewhere that documents what's actually happening in the scene and gives visibility to artists who deserve it without requiring them to compromise what makes them interesting. For a region like Orange County that often gets written off as suburban and culturally shallow, having documentation of this underground community is an act of cultural legitimacy. We exist, we're doing something real, and KEEPITIL is part of how that gets known.
What would you say to someone in Anaheim who wants to get involved in the underground scene but doesn't know where to start?
Start by showing up to events and paying attention to who else is there consistently — the community is accessible if you approach it with genuine interest rather than trying to fast-track your way into relevance. Follow local artists on social media, talk to people at events, and don't be too precious about starting small. The underground scene in Anaheim is full of people who got their start in someone's backyard or at a small warehouse party, and that origin story is something to embrace rather than be embarrassed about. Everyone starts from zero, and the scene rewards the people who do the work over time.
