★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

CLAWZ

303 and Fury

Los Angeles, CA
Acid Techno / Hard Techno
KEEPITIL Artist

The 303 synthesizer was a mistake that became a revolution. CLAWZ understands this deeply. Los Angeles-based and committed to Acid Techno and Hard Techno in equal measure, CLAWZ builds sets around the squelching, resonant, almost alien sound of the 303 running underneath hard, industrial rhythms. It's not easy music. It's necessary music. LA's underground rave rooms have been the testing ground.

"Acid Techno is what happens when a machine has a feeling and doesn't know what to do with it."
THE STORY
How did you first discover Acid Techno, and what's the 303 lineage that got you there?
It started with Chicago — the early acid house records from 1986 and 1987, Phuture's "Acid Tracks," the way that Roland TB-303 bass synthesizer was never supposed to make that sound but made it anyway when you pushed the resonance and the cutoff filter past the point of control. From Chicago acid house it was a straight line to the early UK acid techno scene of the late nineties — labels like Stay Up Forever and Evolution Records, artists like Chris Liberator and Liberator DJs who took the squelch and made it harder, faster, more aggressive without losing the psychedelic quality that makes acid music feel genuinely altered. That lineage from Chicago to London is the DNA of everything I do. The 303 is not a bass synthesizer. It's a portal.
Why is Hard Techno a natural pairing with Acid — what do they share?
Both genres are built on confrontation — on sounds that initially resist you before they pull you in. Hard Techno's industrial kick patterns and compressed, grinding textures create a physical pressure in the low end that the 303's upper-register acid lines cut through perfectly — there's a contrast there, between the weight of the kick and the almost vocal quality of the 303 wail, that creates tension in the mix in a way that neither genre achieves alone. They're also both essentially about machine aesthetics — about celebrating the fact that humans designed instruments and then those instruments did something the humans didn't intend. Hard Techno and Acid Techno are both genres that emerged from accidents and ran with them.
What's LA's underground rave scene like for Hard Techno and Acid right now?
LA's underground is genuinely exciting right now — there's a generation of younger ravers who are connecting with Hard Techno and Acid in a way that feels organic rather than trend-driven, people who found these sounds through genuine curiosity rather than because an algorithm told them to. The warehouse scene specifically has space for harder, more confrontational music in a way that club rooms sometimes don't — when you're not dealing with drink minimums and bottle service, you can book sounds that challenge rather than just satisfy. The community around KEEPITIL has been a big part of building that culture, giving artists like me a context and a platform that connects us to each other and to the people who actually care about these sounds.
Who are the artists that shaped your sound most directly?
Chris Liberator is a foundational influence — his commitment to acid over decades, his refusal to chase trends, his understanding of the 303 as an instrument with unlimited expressive range. Dave Clarke for the way he bridges industrial European techno with the acid tradition and treats DJ sets as compositional works rather than playlist performances. Luke Slater for the sheer sonic intensity of his productions and his ability to layer complex rhythmic structures without losing the essential physicality of the music. And Surgeon — because nobody in techno understands space and tension the way Surgeon does, and Acid Techno without space and tension is just noise at high volume.
THE CRAFT
How do you use the 303 sound in a DJ set — are you bringing hardware or working with recordings?
Both, depending on the setup and the context. In a fully equipped booth I'll run a hardware 303 or a clone alongside the DJ setup — the ability to manipulate the cutoff and resonance in real time, to make the acid line respond to the energy in the room rather than playing out a fixed recording, is a completely different kind of performance. When the setup doesn't allow for hardware, I'm extremely selective about which acid recordings I use — I want ones where the 303 has clearly been played live rather than programmed to a fixed pattern, where you can hear the human decisions in the way the filter moves. A static acid line is just a sound effect. A live acid performance is a conversation.
What does Acid add to Hard Techno that pure Hard Techno can't have alone?
Emotion, or something close to it. Pure Hard Techno can be an extraordinary physical experience — the pressure, the impact, the relentless forward momentum — but it can also become monotonous in a way that's about endurance rather than feeling. Acid introduces what I'd call vocal quality, a sense that the machine is attempting expression, reaching for something beyond rhythm. The 303 wails. It squelches. It has a quality that reads as emotional even though it's entirely electronic, which creates a different kind of engagement from the crowd — you're not just receiving physical impact, you're receiving something that feels like communication from a non-human source. That combination of physical force and almost emotional texture is what makes Acid Techno more complete for me than either genre alone.
How do you balance accessibility with the confrontational nature of the music?
You don't compromise the music to make it accessible — you build the context that makes it make sense. A Hard Techno or Acid Techno set that begins with the most confrontational material in the bag will lose the room before it finds them. The craft is in the journey — starting from somewhere that has enough familiar dance floor energy to invite the listener in, and then moving gradually into more intense, more challenging territory as the room's trust is established. By the time you're playing the most extreme material in a well-built set, the crowd has been prepared for it by everything that came before. They don't just tolerate it — they need it. That's the difference between confrontational music that works and confrontational music that clears a room.
What's your approach to record selection and digging — how do you find new material?
I dig obsessively in the UK acid techno catalog going back to the early nineties — there are releases from that era that almost nobody is playing in 2024 that sound completely fresh because the genre has been out of fashion long enough to lose its cliches. For contemporary material I follow a small number of labels whose quality control I trust — Raving Robotics, Amelie Lens's Exhale, and a few smaller operations that specialize in the hard and acid space. I also pay close attention to what's coming out of Europe, particularly Germany and the UK, where Acid Techno has maintained a continuous underground presence rather than experiencing the genre cycles that have shaped the US market.
THE SCENE
What makes an LA rave room right for this music — what are you looking for in a venue?
Sound system above everything else — Acid Techno is destroyed by inadequate bass reproduction and muddy mid-range. The 303 sits in a frequency range that requires clear, articulate speakers to translate properly; on a bad system it just sounds like noise. Beyond the sound system, I want a room that has physical enclosure, that creates pressure rather than dissipating it — small to medium warehouses, rooms with concrete or brick rather than soft materials that absorb the impact. The social dynamic also matters: rooms with an atmosphere of permission, where people feel free to move and respond to the music without performing for each other, consistently produce better energy for hard and acid sets than spaces defined by self-consciousness.
What does the KEEPITIL community mean to artists working in the underground?
KEEPITIL is doing something that matters more than it might look like from the outside — it's creating a documented record of California underground electronic music at a moment when that scene is genuinely interesting and genuinely underserved by mainstream music media. For artists like me, being part of KEEPITIL means having work that exists beyond the Instagram post cycle, that can be found by someone who searches for Acid Techno in LA three years from now and discovers an actual community rather than just a few scattered profiles. The platform gives the underground continuity and visibility without requiring it to become something other than underground.
What's next for you — where do you want to take the music?
More live hardware performance integrated into DJ sets — the ability to manipulate the 303 and other synthesis in real time changes what a DJ set can be, moves it closer to a live performance without requiring the structural preparation of a full live show. I'm also working on original production, trying to contribute to the Acid Techno catalog rather than just playing from it. And geographically, I want to play more shows outside LA — in the Inland Empire, in San Diego, in Northern California — to connect California's underground communities that currently operate in relative isolation from each other. KEEPITIL is part of making that kind of connection possible.
