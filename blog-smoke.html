★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

Smoke

Every Genre Has a Home in the Smoke

Westminster, CA
House / Minimal / Techno / Afro House / Latin House
KEEPITIL Artist

Westminster sits at the heart of Orange County, and Smoke operates at the heart of Westminster's underground music scene. A DJ who treats genre like a suggestion rather than a rule, Smoke moves between House's warmth, Minimal's precision, Techno's force, Afro House's percussion, and Latin House's groove with the ease of someone who learned music as a language before they learned it as a label. The result is sets that resist easy categorization — they move through moods and frequencies the way the best music always has, guided by feel rather than formula. In a region where DJs often pick a lane and stay in it, Smoke has built something rarer: a sound that is entirely its own.

"The smoke doesn't choose a direction. It just fills the room."
THE STORY
How did you get your start playing venues in Orange County?
It started the way it starts for most people — small rooms, late nights, and a lot of time spent watching other DJs do it before I ever touched a pair of decks myself. Orange County has a real underground if you know where to look, and I found it by going to every party I could find and just absorbing everything. My first real sets were at small house events in Westminster and Garden Grove, maybe fifty people at most, but those rooms taught me more than any big stage ever could. You learn to read a crowd when the crowd is close enough to read back.
How has Afro House influenced the way you mix?
Afro House changed everything about how I think about percussion. When I first started going deep into producers like Black Coffee and the South African scene, I realized I had been underestimating what rhythm could carry on its own — the way a single layered drum pattern could hold an entire room without needing a drop or a big synth line to justify itself. That patience entered everything I do. Now, even when I'm playing straight Techno or Minimal, there's a rhythmic consciousness underneath it that I think comes directly from the time I spent studying Afro House. You can hear Africa in it even when you're not playing African music.
How does Minimal Techno contrast with Latin House in one of your sets?
They're actually not as far apart as people assume — both have this insistence on groove over spectacle, on letting the beat do the heavy lifting rather than flooding the mix with layers. What changes is the texture and the cultural memory. Minimal Techno pulls from a kind of industrial austerity, from Berlin and Detroit and the idea that less is architecturally more. Latin House has warmth baked into its DNA — congas, brass hits, the feel of a dance floor that knows how to move together. When I transition between them in a set, I'm not jumping between opposites. I'm sliding between two different temperatures of the same philosophy.
What does Westminster give you as a base for underground music?
Westminster is genuinely underrated as a cultural hub. You've got one of the largest Vietnamese communities in the country here, an incredibly diverse population, and a long history of people who came from places with serious music traditions. That bleeds into everything, including the underground scene. When I play for a Westminster crowd, I'm playing for people whose ears have been shaped by sounds from across the entire world, and that gives me permission to be genre-fluid in a way that a more homogeneous crowd might not allow. The city itself teaches you to hold multiple things at once.
THE CRAFT
Who are your biggest influences and what did you take from each?
Black Coffee taught me that soul and minimalism are not opposites — that restraint can be the most emotional choice a DJ makes. DJ Stingray showed me that you can be completely technical and completely funky at the same time, that the machine and the human don't have to be at war. Louie Vega is the master of making a room feel like a family, of using House music as a kind of communal ritual rather than just entertainment. And Kerri Chandler is the reason I understand sub-bass as a spiritual element — the way he uses low frequencies to make people feel something they cannot name. Between those four, I have most of what I need.
What is your actual mixing approach when you're genre-fluid — how do you plan that?
I don't plan it, exactly. I come in with a general emotional arc in mind — I want to take this room from X to Y over the course of four hours — and then I let the crowd tell me what tools to use. The genre becomes the tool, not the destination. If the room needs warmth, I reach for House. If it needs structure, I reach for Minimal. If it needs fire, Techno. If it needs to breathe and swing, Afro House. If it needs to feel alive in its hips, Latin House. I think of myself less as a genre DJ and more as a room reader who happens to have a very large vocabulary.
How do you handle transitions between genres that sound very different?
The secret is that you almost never jump directly — you build a bridge. Every genre has a version of itself that leans toward another genre, and your job is to find those liminal tracks. There are House records that have a Techno edge. There are Minimal records that feel like Latin percussion if you listen to the right layer. You sequence through those bridge tracks and nobody notices the transition because you've given their ears a ramp instead of a cliff. When it works right, people just feel the energy shift and assume you were always headed there.
What gear defines your setup?
I play on CDJs almost exclusively at this point, though I came up on vinyl and that will always be in my hands even if it's no longer in my crates at most shows. The hardware doesn't define me — the library does. I spend more time on music selection than on any technical element of DJing, because at the end of a night nobody remembers whether your transitions were clean or sloppy. They remember whether the music made them feel something. So I am obsessively, constantly adding to and curating my library, across all five genres, looking for the records that are doing something the other records aren't.
THE SCENE
What is the state of Orange County's underground electronic scene right now?
It's growing in a way that feels real rather than trend-driven, which is the best possible thing to say about any underground scene. There are more promoters taking risks on deeper music, more venues willing to stay open late for the right crowd, and a new generation of DJs who are genuinely studying the history rather than just copying what's popular right now. OC has always lived in LA's shadow culturally, but that might actually be an advantage — it means the people who do build something here are doing it out of genuine love, not because it's the path of least resistance.
What advice would you give to DJs who want to go genre-fluid but feel pressure to specialize?
I'd say the pressure to specialize is real and comes from a real place — it's easier to market a single-genre DJ, easier to book them, easier to explain what they do. But that pressure serves the industry more than it serves the music. If you genuinely hear connections between genres, you owe it to yourself to follow those connections wherever they go. The artists who last are almost never the ones who played it safest. Build your craft deeply in each genre before you try to mix them — genre fluency only works if you've actually put in the hours in each language you're speaking.
What does KEEPITIL mean to you as a platform?
KEEPITIL represents the underground ethos that I think California genuinely has but doesn't always get credit for. There's a tendency to write off Southern California as a commercial music market — and sure, there's plenty of that here — but there's also a genuine underground that has been running for decades, quietly, away from the spotlight, and KEEPITIL is one of the places putting a name and a face to that. Getting to be part of that feels like the right kind of recognition — not the loud kind, but the kind that actually means something.
