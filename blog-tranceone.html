TranceOne

Trance Roots, Techno Future

Santa Clarita, CA
Tech Trance / Hard Techno
KEEPITIL Artist

Santa Clarita doesn't exactly scream rave capital — but TranceOne has been quietly building something. A DJ who moves between Tech Trance's melodic euphoria and Hard Techno's industrial momentum, TranceOne operates in a space where both genres find common ground: the relentless forward motion that makes dance floors surrender. The sets land somewhere between a spiritual experience and a physical beatdown. Usually both at once. What makes TranceOne distinctive isn't the genre range itself — it's the deep knowledge of why Trance and Techno have always been closer than their respective audiences want to admit, and the skill to make that relationship audible in real time.

"Trance gave me the melody. Techno gave me the teeth."
THE STORY
Where did your journey into Trance music begin, and what were the formative records?
Trance hit me early and it hit me hard. Ferry Corsten's "Sunset on Ibiza" was one of those early revelations — the way that track builds and releases is almost mathematical in its elegance, but it lands with pure emotion when you're in the right state to receive it. Tiësto in his early era, particularly the "In My Memory" album, showed me what it meant to take a crowd on a journey that lasted hours rather than minutes. Markus Schulz was important because he always pushed Trance toward the harder, darker end of the spectrum, which was the direction my own taste was eventually going to go. Those three artists together gave me a complete education in what melodic electronic music could be when it was played by people who truly understood it.
How did you make the transition toward Hard Techno from a Trance background?
It didn't feel like a transition so much as a natural evolution. I started getting drawn to the darker edges of Tech Trance — the tracks that stripped away the saccharine melodic elements and left something harder and more industrial underneath. From there it was a short jump to Hard Techno proper, which shares the relentless forward momentum of Trance but replaces the melodic euphoria with a different kind of intensity — more gritty, more aggressive, more physically confrontational. The BPM ranges also overlap more than people expect, which makes blending the genres in a live set less jarring than you might think. Trance prepared me perfectly for Hard Techno because they're both about the relentless build toward release.
What's it like building a career based in Santa Clarita, which isn't typically part of the LA electronic music narrative?
Santa Clarita is in the northern part of LA County, separated from the main urban core by mountains and a significant commute. That distance from the center of LA's scene has meant that I've had to be proactive about making connections and getting into rooms rather than having them fall into my lap organically. On the other hand, it's meant that when I show up to play a set in central LA or in the OC underground, I'm coming from somewhere that requires a commitment — which perhaps gives the sets a different energy, a kind of earned presence. I've also found that the Santa Clarita Valley has its own music community that doesn't always get acknowledged in the broader LA conversation, and being part of that local culture matters to me.
What do you want people who've never heard Tech Trance to know about the genre?
Tech Trance is probably the most misunderstood genre in electronic music. People hear "Trance" and assume they know what it is — and the mainstream commercial Trance that got big in the early 2000s is a real thing, but Tech Trance is something entirely different. It's harder, darker, more minimal in its melodic approach, with a driving rhythmic intensity that has more in common with industrial music than with anything playing on commercial radio. The "Tech" prefix exists precisely because it marks out a different tradition. If you've dismissed Trance based on what you've heard in popular culture, you've almost certainly never actually heard Tech Trance done properly, and that's worth correcting.
THE CRAFT
How do you blend Tech Trance and Hard Techno in a live set without the transition feeling jarring?
The key is finding the emotional through-line rather than trying to match genres technically. Both Tech Trance and Hard Techno are built around sustained energy and forward momentum — they're both genres that don't let up, that refuse to give the crowd a complete rest once the set is in motion. I use the melodic elements of Trance as a counterpoint to the industrial aggression of Hard Techno, so the set breathes in a way that pure Hard Techno sets sometimes don't. A sweeping Trance lead after thirty minutes of Hard Techno hits differently than either would on its own — the contrast amplifies both. I spend a lot of time in preparation finding tracks on both sides of the spectrum that can live in the same set without clashing.
What does Tech Trance offer emotionally that Hard Techno doesn't have?
Tech Trance offers uplift and melodic release in a way that's almost impossible to replicate in Hard Techno. When a Tech Trance lead melody comes in after a period of heavy, dark Techno, the emotional response in a room is palpable — people raise their hands, their faces change, there's a visible shift in energy that goes from internal to external, from endurance to celebration. That's a particular power that melodic music has over purely rhythmic music, and it's one I'm not willing to give up by committing exclusively to Hard Techno. The combination lets me create sets that move between different emotional registers rather than camping in one intensity level for the entire duration.
How do you structure a set that takes the crowd on a journey across both genres?
I think of a set like a piece of architecture that has different rooms. The entry point is important — I usually start with something that establishes mood and energy without fully revealing the direction the set will take. The first major genre shift, from whichever direction I'm coming from, is planned to happen at a moment when the crowd has settled in enough to be surprised by it. The peak of the set is typically a Hard Techno section because the physicality of that genre creates the most intense collective experience, and then I use the return to more melodic Tech Trance territory as a kind of emotional resolution. Not every set follows that structure exactly, but that arc — tension, peak, release — is always the underlying intention.
THE SCENE
How do you see the LA underground receiving the combination of Trance and Hard Techno?
LA is an interesting place to play this combination because the city has strong communities around both genres that don't overlap much historically. The Trance community here is real and dedicated, with deep roots going back to the late '90s and early 2000s scene. The Hard Techno community is newer and more aggressive, drawing from a younger demographic. Playing sets that bridge both creates this interesting cross-pollination where Trance fans get introduced to the intensity of Hard Techno, and Techno fans discover that Trance at its best isn't the cheese they might have assumed. That genre education function that DJs can serve is something I take seriously as a value I bring to a night.
What's your advice to electronic music artists coming from a Trance background who want to push into harder territory?
Don't abandon what you know — the skills you developed playing Trance are genuinely valuable in a Hard Techno context. Understanding how to build a long, sustained journey, how to use melodic elements to create emotional peaks, how to manage a crowd's energy across hours rather than just minutes: all of those Trance-developed skills translate directly to being a better Hard Techno DJ. The artists who make the Trance-to-Techno bridge work are usually the ones who bring both sensibilities to both genres rather than treating them as entirely separate traditions. Respect your roots and your new direction will be stronger for it.
What are you working toward in the next phase of TranceOne's journey?
I want to put out original productions that genuinely capture what the Tech Trance / Hard Techno hybrid sounds like when it's done with intention — not just mixing genres but finding the space where they actually merge into something new. I'm also focused on building a more consistent live presence across Southern California and eventually beyond, reaching the rooms and cities where this combination of sounds will resonate with audiences who haven't heard it yet. KEEPITIL has been an important platform for connecting with the underground network, and I plan to keep deepening that connection as both of our networks grow. The mission is the same as it's always been: bring the melody and the teeth together in the same room.
