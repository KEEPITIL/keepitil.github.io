★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

RAVE GOD

The Altar Is the Dance Floor

Orange County, CA
Underground Electronic
KEEPITIL Artist

The name isn't a boast — it's a philosophy. RAVE GOD operates in Orange County's underground electronic scene with the conviction that a rave isn't just a party, it's a ritual. The dance floor is a sacred space. The music is the ceremony. Operating in OC's underground circuit, RAVE GOD brings an intensity and intentionality to every set that the name demands. The crowd comes expecting something. They always get it.

"A rave isn't what happens when music plays. It's what happens when the right music plays in the right room with the right people."
THE STORY
What's the philosophy behind the name RAVE GOD — is it a statement, a joke, or something else entirely?
It's a statement that contains a joke about statements. The name is deliberately maximalist, almost self-parodying — but underneath the provocation is a real conviction that rave culture deserves to be taken with the same seriousness that we give to any tradition of communal spiritual practice. A "god" in the theological sense is something that mediates between the human and the transcendent, and that's exactly what a great DJ does in a great room: creates a space where ordinary reality is suspended and something larger becomes accessible. The name is arrogant the way that calling yourself a priest is arrogant — it's not a claim of personal superiority, it's a claim of function and commitment. I take the function seriously. The name is the daily reminder to deserve it.
What does rave culture mean to you beyond just entertainment — what is it actually for?
Rave culture at its origin was one of the most sophisticated collective technologies for producing altered states of consciousness that modern Western culture has developed — and it did it with music, with light, with the specific social dynamics of a crowd of strangers choosing to share space and sound together without the usual social hierarchies enforcing themselves. The entertainment reading of rave culture misses almost everything important about it. It's a practice of collective ecstasy, of losing and finding yourself simultaneously in a group experience, of temporarily dissolving the boundaries between your private self and the community around you. Every tradition of collective spiritual practice does something like this. Rave does it without requiring specific metaphysical commitments. That's what makes it so powerful and so widely accessible.
How did you connect with rave culture — what was the formative experience?
My first proper rave — not a club night, not a festival, a real underground rave with a flyer you had to know someone to get and a location that wasn't announced until hours before — was the experience that split my life into before and after. The physical scale of it, the darkness and the light, the sound system doing things to my body that I hadn't known sound could do, the sense of the crowd as a single entity rather than a collection of individuals — none of that was prepared for by anything in ordinary life. I went in as someone curious about electronic music and came out as someone who understood that this was not just music but a form of collective human experience that I needed to be part of, not just as an attendee but as someone who could help create it for others.
What's the OC underground circuit like — what venues and communities are part of it?
OC's underground circuit is smaller than LA's but more tightly knit — there's a genuine community here, people who know each other across events and years, who've built relationships through the shared experience of seeking out underground music together. The circuit runs through a mix of permanent venues in Santa Ana and Anaheim that have relationships with underground promoters, and a rotating cast of one-time spaces — warehouses, outdoor locations, art spaces — that provide the illegal or semi-legal thrill that is genuinely part of the rave tradition's appeal. KEEPITIL's network connects the OC underground to the broader California scene in ways that have made it possible to bring artists from the Inland Empire and LA to OC and vice versa, which strengthens both communities.
THE CRAFT
What sets your sets apart from a typical club night — what's different about a RAVE GOD performance?
Intention, primarily. A typical club night is a service transaction: the promoter books a DJ, the DJ plays music, the crowd dances, everyone goes home. What I'm trying to create is a ritual arc — an evening that has a beginning, a transformation in the middle, and a return at the end, where the people who attended have been somewhere that ordinary recreational activity doesn't take them. That requires a different kind of preparation, a different relationship with the music, and a willingness to prioritize the collective experience over individual moments of spectacle. The best sets I've ever played are the ones where the crowd was so immersed that the DJ — me — became irrelevant, where the music was just happening to everyone in the room together. That's the goal every time.
What artists from rave culture's history have most shaped your approach?
The Prodigy taught me that rave music could have the force and legitimacy of any rock or punk tradition — that it didn't need to apologize for its intensity or its populism. Sasha and Digweed's Northern Exposure mix showed me what DJ craft at its highest level looks like: sets with genuine compositional logic, where the relationship between tracks creates something larger than the sum of its parts, where a four-hour mix is one continuous piece of music rather than a sequence of songs. Early Daft Punk — Homework and the records around it — for demonstrating that rave aesthetics could be both emotionally intelligent and physically irresistible, that depth and power were not in opposition. These artists are why the culture is worth sustaining.
How do you think about the spiritual dimension of what you're doing — how does that translate into practical decisions in the booth?
In practical terms, the spiritual intention translates into patience. When I frame a set as ritual rather than entertainment, I'm committing to allowing the ritual to unfold at the pace it requires rather than the pace that would maximize moment-to-moment excitement. A ritual has stages — preparation, entry, transformation, integration — and those stages require time. I'm willing to spend thirty minutes building the conditions for a peak that will last ten, because the meaning of the peak comes from what preceded it. I'm also attuned to collective states in a way that pure entertainment doesn't require: I'm watching the room not just to see whether people are dancing but to see whether they're in a shared state or in parallel individual states. The goal is to move them from the latter to the former.
What makes a rave fail — what are the conditions that prevent the magic from happening?
Distraction and self-consciousness, primarily. The conditions that allow a rave to work — the surrender to collective experience, the willingness to move without monitoring your own movement, the dissolution of the usual social boundaries — are destroyed by environments that reintroduce ordinary social pressure. Venues with VIP sections, promoters who book based on follower counts rather than musical quality, sound systems that are impressive as objects but don't create the physical experience that rave requires — all of these are forms of distraction that keep people in their ordinary social selves rather than moving them into the collective state that rave is designed to produce. The underground's resistance to commercial logic is not just aesthetic preference. It's the practical protection of the conditions that allow the music to work.
THE SCENE
What's your vision for OC's underground scene — where should it go in the next few years?
More infrastructure, more permanence, more documentation. OC's underground has always been somewhat transient — events happen, communities form, and then the spaces close or the promoters move on and the accumulated culture disperses. What I want to see is the kind of continuity that makes a scene recognizable over time: venues that have a sustained relationship with underground programming, promoters who build careers here rather than using OC as a stepping stone to LA, artists who identify as OC underground artists rather than as people waiting to break out of OC. KEEPITIL's work of documentation and community-building is part of creating that continuity, giving the scene a narrative it can reference and build on rather than reinventing itself from zero every few years.
What do you want people who've never been to an underground rave to understand about why it matters?
That it's one of the few places left in contemporary culture where strangers can have a genuine shared experience rather than parallel individual ones. Most entertainment is consumed individually even when it's happening in a crowd — cinema, concerts, sports events are all fundamentally about your personal experience of something, with the crowd as context rather than as co-creator. A rave at its best is something different: the crowd is not the context for the experience, the crowd is the experience, and your individual presence is one element of a collective event that couldn't happen without all of you together. That kind of genuine togetherness is increasingly rare and increasingly important in a culture that has become skilled at making people feel connected while actually keeping them isolated.
What does being part of the KEEPITIL network mean for the work you're doing in OC?
Legitimacy and connection. Legitimacy because being featured on KEEPITIL positions the work within a larger California underground context that gives it meaning beyond the local — when someone outside OC encounters RAVE GOD through KEEPITIL, they encounter it as part of a genuine scene rather than as an isolated profile. Connection because the KEEPITIL network links me to artists across Southern California who share the values and the commitment that make the underground worth being part of — Aztekan in San Bernardino, RA1N in Temecula, Oscar Osorio in Santa Ana, all the artists across the California underground who are doing serious work in their cities. That network is the infrastructure of a real scene, and KEEPITIL is building it one artist page at a time.
