★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

FUSION x

Everything Blends When the Timing Is Right

Orange County, CA
Underground Electronic
KEEPITIL Artist

The name is the mission statement. FUSION x doesn't subscribe to one genre, one BPM range, or one energy level. Operating in Orange County's underground circuit, this DJ brings a genuine blending approach to sets that keeps rooms engaged by keeping them constantly surprised. The fusion is real — not in the confused, everything-at-once sense that can feel chaotic rather than creative, but in the skilled, well-timed way that makes each genre transition feel inevitable in retrospect, as though the second track was always where the first track was heading. This is a level of technical and curatorial mastery that takes real time to develop, and FUSION x has put in that time. OC's underground circuit has benefited from having a DJ who treats genre boundaries as tools rather than constraints, someone who can take a crowd from one emotional and sonic territory to another without losing the thread of what makes a great set feel coherent. In a scene that tends to organize itself around distinct subgenres and their loyal audiences, FUSION x offers something different: a shared musical experience that draws everyone in.

"The best transition is the one nobody sees coming but everyone feels was right."
THE STORY
What does fusion mean to you in your DJing approach — how do you define it in practice?
Fusion means I'm treating the set as a single piece of music rather than a playlist of separate songs in a single style. Each track I choose is responding to everything that came before it and setting up everything that comes after it, and those relationships can cross genre lines freely if the underlying energy connection is right. In practice it means I might move from a deep house groove into something with a more industrial edge, or from a hip-hop influenced break into a piece of techno, because I'm following the emotional logic of the set rather than the genre rules. The x in my name represents that unknown variable — the thing that makes any particular fusion work, which is the right feeling in the right room at the right moment.
Tell us about the OC underground scene and where you fit into it?
OC's underground has a lot of energy and a strong community that spans a pretty wide geographic area — from Anaheim and Orange to cities further south like Mission Viejo. The scene tends to cluster around specific subgenre loyalties, which creates a lot of depth in each corner but can also lead to a certain fragmentation where people don't often see how connected the different parts of the underground actually are. I see my role partly as a bridge builder in that sense — because I move across genres in my sets, I tend to pull audiences from different corners of the underground and mix them together in the same room. That cross-pollination is healthy for the scene, and I think FUSION x sets have become associated with that kind of mixed, open-minded energy.
How did you develop a genre-fluid style — was this always your instinct or did it evolve?
It evolved from a very genuine place: I just couldn't bring myself to only listen to one kind of music, and when I started DJing I found I couldn't bring myself to only play one kind either. Early sets were probably less polished in how the blending worked — the transitions were more obvious, the genre shifts more jarring — but over time I developed the ear and the technical skill to make movements across genre lines feel smooth and intentional. The breakthrough was realizing that tempo and key aren't the only things that connect tracks — energy, texture, and emotional tone are just as important, and you can find connections along those dimensions between tracks that are technically very different. That opened up the whole map for me.
What do your musical influences look like across the spectrum you draw from?
The influences are genuinely wide. On the electronic side, I've been shaped by everything from the Chicago house tradition to UK garage to contemporary techno and everything in between. Beyond pure electronic music, I take a lot from hip-hop production — the sample culture and the texture work that goes into great beats — and from genres like drum and bass and jungle that have always been inherently fusion forms, combining influences from reggae, jazz, funk, and electronic music into something new. I'm also influenced by the producers who are explicitly genre-fluid — artists who have built careers on the idea that music doesn't have to choose a lane. Those influences come through in how I approach curation and mixing, even when the specific music I'm playing sounds different from those sources.
THE CRAFT
What are the technical challenges of blending across styles, and how do you navigate them?
The core technical challenge is that different genres have different conventions around tempo, key, and structure — the way a house track is built is genuinely different from the way a drum and bass track is built, so the toolkit for transitioning between them can't be identical to what you'd use within a genre. I've had to develop a much larger technical vocabulary than a genre-specialist DJ needs, because I might need to make any number of different kinds of transitions in a single set. The key realization is that energy management is the universal language — whatever technical approach you use, the goal is always to maintain the momentum of the room through the transition, and if you succeed at that, the specific technical method becomes secondary. I practice a lot, and I listen back to recordings of my sets to evaluate what worked and what didn't at the transition level.
How do you prepare for a set knowing you might go in multiple genre directions?
Preparation for a fusion set is more about having a large, well-organized library with multiple potential paths through it than about having a fixed tracklist. I identify clusters of music that work well together across genre lines, what I think of as fusion nodes — tracks that have a foot in two different worlds and can serve as pivot points in the set. From those nodes I can go in multiple directions depending on what the room is telling me. I also think about the emotional arc I want to travel rather than the genre sequence I want to move through, because the emotional logic is what the audience actually feels — they don't experience "and now we're transitioning from techno to house," they experience the feeling of the room shifting in a particular direction.
What do you want audiences to walk away feeling after a FUSION x set?
I want them to feel like they traveled somewhere — like the two hours or three hours of the set took them through a genuine musical journey with distinct territories and memorable moments rather than just a sustained exposure to a single vibe. The best feedback I've gotten is from people who say they didn't realize how much time had passed, which means they were fully engaged throughout and the set didn't allow for that moment of "okay, I know where this is going." I also want people to walk away having heard something they hadn't heard before, or heard something familiar in a new context that made it feel different. That's what fusion at its best can do — reframe existing music through the context of unexpected pairings.
How do you stay current and keep your library fresh across multiple genres?
Staying current across multiple genres is genuinely demanding — it's multiple times the listening and curation work of staying current in one genre. I approach it systematically: I have regular listening sessions dedicated to each of the genre areas I draw from, and I try to stay connected to people whose taste in each area I trust so I get recommendations that I might not find on my own. The connective tissue between genres is where I spend the most creative energy — not just finding great tracks in each genre, but finding the tracks that have cross-genre potential, the ones that feel like they exist in a liminal space between styles. Those tracks are the building blocks of fusion sets, and finding them is some of the most satisfying curatorial work I do.
THE SCENE
What does OC's underground rave culture offer that you can't find in mainstream venues?
The underground offers the freedom to take the audience somewhere unexpected, which is the whole foundation of what I'm trying to do with fusion sets. In a mainstream venue, the expectation management is built into the booking — you know what genre the DJ plays before you get there, and the event is designed around delivering that specific thing. In the underground, there's more of a shared agreement that the DJ is going to lead you somewhere, and you're going to trust the process without knowing exactly where you'll end up. That trust is the thing that makes ambitious, genre-crossing sets possible. Without it, fusion doesn't work — you need an audience that's willing to follow you through the unexpected transitions rather than expecting you to stay in their comfort zone.
What has the KEEPITIL community meant for your development as an artist?
Being part of the KEEPITIL roster has given me a context that helps people understand what I'm doing. When you're a genre-fluid DJ, it can be hard to find a category that fits you — booking systems want to know what you play, and "everything" is not an answer that works in most contexts. KEEPITIL's framing around the OC and California underground is broad enough to accommodate what I do, and the community of artists on the roster who are also doing non-standard things makes me feel less like an outlier and more like one approach among many in a genuinely diverse underground scene. That sense of belonging to a community that values variety is creatively energizing.
Where do you see genre-fusion in underground electronic music heading as a broader movement?
I think we're in a moment where genre boundaries in electronic music are becoming less rigid across the board, not just in underground contexts. The streaming era has exposed listeners to much wider ranges of music than previous generations experienced, and that's producing a generation of listeners who don't have strong genre loyalty in the traditional sense — they follow feeling and energy rather than stylistic categories. That's the most hospitable environment for fusion DJing that has ever existed, and I think the next decade of underground electronic music is going to see increasingly fluid approaches become the norm rather than the exception. Being part of OC's underground during this transition feels like being in exactly the right place at the right time.
