★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

DD Balenziaga

Fashion Forward, Bass First

Orange County, CA
Underground Electronic
KEEPITIL Artist

The name nods to fashion. The music is pure underground. DD Balenziaga operates in the intersection between culture and sound — an OC underground DJ who understands that a set is not just heard, it's experienced from the moment you walk through the door to the moment you leave, and that aesthetic is part of the total picture of what makes an experience land. The reference to Balenciaga is knowing and intentional: it signals an awareness of culture, of how things look and feel and what they communicate, while the DD prefix grounds it in something personal and distinct. Orange County's underground circuit has embraced this perspective enthusiastically, because it matches something true about how the best underground events work — music that hits hard and an environment that looks good doing it are not competing values, they're complementary ones. DD Balenziaga brings both without sacrificing either, and the result is a presence in OC's underground that's hard to miss and harder to forget.

"Underground music deserves to look as good as it sounds."
THE STORY
Where does the name DD Balenziaga come from and what does it represent?
The name came from thinking about what I wanted my presence in the scene to communicate beyond just the music. Balenciaga as a reference point carries a specific set of associations — high-low culture collision, avant-garde sensibility packaged in something instantly recognizable, a kind of aggressive confidence about aesthetics that doesn't apologize for caring about how things look. The DD is personal, a prefix that makes the reference mine rather than just a borrowed association. Together the name is meant to signal that this project exists at the intersection of cultural awareness and underground music, that it's thinking about the full experience rather than just the sonic one. It's a name that asks questions before you've even heard a track, which is exactly what I want.
How do aesthetics and underground music intersect in your work?
Underground music has always had a visual and cultural dimension that's inseparable from the sonic one — the flyers, the fashion at events, the spaces where music happens are all part of a total aesthetic experience that shapes how the music lands. I'm just more intentional about that connection than some DJs, who treat the visual and cultural layer as secondary or irrelevant. When I put together a set, I'm thinking about the whole environment that set exists in — what the night looks like, how the space feels, what people are wearing and projecting. Music doesn't exist in a vacuum; it exists in a context, and that context is as much aesthetic as acoustic. Being aware of that isn't superficial — it's a deeper engagement with what electronic music culture actually is.
Tell us about your relationship to OC's underground scene and how you found your place in it?
OC's underground found me as much as I found it — I was already deep in electronic music culture when I started going to events in the county, and the community here resonated with me because it had the right values: real music, real people, genuine shared investment in the culture. My place in the scene has solidified over time through showing up consistently and bringing a perspective that added something the scene didn't already have — that explicit awareness of aesthetic as part of the musical conversation. The community has been receptive because they're sophisticated enough to appreciate that dimension, and it's contributed to the overall richness of what OC's underground offers culturally. Being part of something that's genuinely growing and developing is one of the most satisfying things about being in this scene.
What was your path into DJing — how did you get started?
I came in through the music obsessive route rather than the performer route — I was a compulsive listener and collector before I ever thought about DJing as something I would do rather than just appreciate. The pivot happened when I realized that the way I organized and moved through music in my own listening was itself a kind of curating and mixing, and that there was a direct line from that private practice to the public practice of DJing. Early on I was figuring out both the technical craft and the performance identity simultaneously, which is challenging but also means those two things developed together rather than one preceding the other. The aesthetic sensibility was always there — it just took the DJ context to find the right form of expression for it.
THE CRAFT
How would you describe your DJ style and the sound direction of your sets?
The sets run hard — I'm not interested in low-energy underground music, and I think the intersection of aesthetic sophistication and heavy bass-forward electronic music is a more interesting territory than either alone. The style draws heavily from the harder ends of house and techno, with influences from UK garage and the club music traditions that have always existed at the intersection of fashion culture and underground music. The key word in my work is intention — every element of a set is chosen rather than defaulted to, and that intentionality is what creates the distinctive texture that I think people associate with my performances. It's music that sounds like someone was paying attention to every detail.
What makes your sets distinctive compared to other DJs in OC's underground?
The aesthetic coherence is probably the most distinctive thing — there's a through line in my sets that goes beyond genre or tempo into something more like a consistent visual and emotional signature. The music I play has a look, if that makes sense, a specific quality that coheres across tracks even when those tracks are technically quite different from each other. That coherence comes from very deliberate curation and from a clear internal aesthetic standard that I'm always selecting toward. In a scene with a lot of technically skilled DJs, the differentiator is often exactly this kind of aesthetic vision — the ability to create an experience that feels unified and intentional rather than just technically correct.
What is your vision for bridging underground music and visual culture?
The vision is for underground music events to be taken seriously as total aesthetic experiences — not just the sound, but the space, the visuals, the fashion, the overall environment that the music exists in. There are precedents for this in club culture history: the clubs and events that became legendary were almost always the ones that understood the full experience, not just the lineup. In OC's underground specifically, there's an opportunity to elevate the total production of events while maintaining the anti-commercial, community-oriented values that make the underground what it is. Those things aren't in tension — you can care deeply about aesthetics without selling out, and I think DD Balenziaga is proof of that proposition in practice.
Who are the artists and cultural figures who have most influenced your perspective?
On the music side, I'm influenced by the producers and DJs who have always operated at the intersection of fashion and electronic music — the UK club scene specifically has a long history of this that I find continuously inspiring. From the rave era through garage and grime and into contemporary club music, UK artists have understood that electronic music culture is a lifestyle and an aesthetic proposition as much as it is a sonic one. Beyond music, fashion designers who approach their work with the same kind of cultural intelligence that the best DJs bring to theirs have been formative for me — people who understand that what you put in the world communicates something, and that communication should be intentional and interesting. That dual influence is what DD Balenziaga is built from.
THE SCENE
How does OC's underground compare to other scenes you've experienced?
OC's underground has a specific character that I find really compelling — it's intimate enough to be genuine but sophisticated enough to appreciate what I'm trying to do with the aesthetic dimension of the work. Scenes that are too small can lack the reference points to recognize and appreciate cultural complexity; scenes that are too large can become impersonal and trend-driven. OC is in a sweet spot where the community has developed real depth of knowledge and taste while maintaining the close-knit character of a scene that's genuinely committed to its values. Playing for OC underground audiences feels like playing for people who are paying attention on multiple levels, which elevates the work for everyone.
What does the KEEPITIL platform mean for the OC underground community?
KEEPITIL is giving the OC underground the documentation and visibility it deserves while keeping the framing rooted in what the scene actually is. The biggest challenge for any underground scene is being seen without being co-opted — having enough visibility that new people can find it without becoming so visible that it loses the qualities that made it worth finding in the first place. KEEPITIL seems to understand that balance, and the way it presents artists like those on its roster treats the underground with the seriousness and intelligence it deserves rather than packaging it for mass consumption. That's a valuable thing, and the DD Balenziaga perspective — that aesthetics matter — is well served by a platform that takes the visual and cultural presentation of the scene seriously.
What do you want people to take away from the DD Balenziaga experience?
I want people to walk away feeling like they experienced something that was fully considered — where every element was there for a reason and the whole thing added up to something greater than the sum of its parts. Underground music at its best isn't just a collection of good tracks played in sequence; it's a complete cultural experience that uses sound as its primary medium but engages every dimension of how we experience being in a space together with other people. If someone leaves a DD Balenziaga event thinking "that was the whole package — the music, the feel, the vibe, all of it working together" then I've done exactly what I set out to do. Fashion forward, bass first: those aren't contradictions, they're two dimensions of the same commitment to doing everything well.
