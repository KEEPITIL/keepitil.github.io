★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

Rex Elvis

Minimal Is the Maximum Statement

Lake Forest, CA
Minimal / Ghetto-Tech / Techno
KEEPITIL Artist

From Lake Forest in Orange County, Rex Elvis carved out a lane where minimalism is maximalism — where fewer elements mean more impact. Ghetto-Tech groove meets Techno precision in sets that feel simultaneously sparse and overwhelmingly present. Southern California's underground rooms have embraced Rex Elvis as proof that restraint can be the loudest thing in the room. The stripped-back approach isn't a stylistic limitation; it's a deliberate philosophy, one built on the radical idea that what you choose not to play matters as much as what you do.

"Take away everything you don't need. What's left is the truth."
THE STORY
How did you discover Minimal Techno, and what was it that hooked you?
The first time I heard genuinely minimal Techno in a proper club context, I thought something was wrong with the sound system — it seemed like so little was happening. But then I realized I was absolutely transfixed by every tiny element, every micro-percussion hit, every barely-there melodic fragment. That moment of recognizing how much power can live in emptiness was the revelation. Minimalism in Techno is not about stripping the music down because you can't fill it — it's about trusting the listener to fill it themselves, which creates a much more participatory experience than music that tells you exactly how to feel at every moment.
What's the connection between Ghetto-Tech and Minimal Techno in your sets — they seem like unlikely partners?
That's what makes it interesting to me — they share more DNA than people expect. Ghetto-Tech, as pioneered by DJ Assault and the Godfather and those early Detroit crews, is fundamentally about rhythm and attitude and an almost confrontational directness. It's stripped of pretension, rooted in the body, and very much about the groove rather than elaborate production. Minimal Techno operates on similar principles from a different cultural origin — both reject the idea that more elements equal more impact. When you blend them, you get something that has Ghetto-Tech's rhythmic rawness and sexual energy combined with Techno's hypnotic, architecture-building quality. The rooms that experience it usually don't know what's hitting them, in the best possible way.
What is it like being based in Lake Forest — not exactly the first city people associate with Techno?
Lake Forest is a suburban city in south Orange County that's about as far from a Techno hotbed as you can imagine, and I think that's actually been formative for me. When you're not surrounded by a pre-existing scene that tells you what you're supposed to sound like, you develop your taste in isolation, which can produce something more idiosyncratic and personal. I spent a lot of time just listening and absorbing before I started performing, and the distance from any particular scene meant I could draw from Detroit Ghetto-Tech and European Minimal Techno simultaneously without feeling like I was violating someone's cultural rules. The commute to LA or deeper into OC for shows is real, but the artistic freedom of that distance has been worth it.
Who are the Ghetto-Tech legends from Detroit that shaped your approach?
DJ Assault is the absolute cornerstone — "Ass-N-Pussy" and everything from that era might seem crude on the surface but it's actually deeply musical in the way it uses rhythm and repetition to build hypnotic momentum. The Godfather showed me how to use minimal elements to create maximum attitude, which is a fundamentally different skill set than what you learn from listening to more "respectable" electronic music. Drexciya, while not strictly Ghetto-Tech, informed my understanding of how Detroit electronic music uses minimalism to create worlds. Those Detroit producers were doing something radical — making music that was both extremely local and extremely futuristic simultaneously.
THE CRAFT
How do you actually build tension with minimal elements — what's the technique behind it?
Tension in Minimal Techno is almost entirely about expectation management. You establish a pattern early in the set, let the crowd's body internalize it, and then you introduce very small variations — a filter opening slightly, a percussion element dropping out for exactly one bar, a bassline shifting by a single note. The crowd processes those tiny changes as enormous because they've been so focused on the pattern. It requires patience and a deep trust in the music — you can't rush the process or overload it with too many changes too quickly. The builds in Minimal Techno are long, often uncomfortably long, because the payoff requires that foundation of accumulated tension to mean anything.
What does Ghetto-Tech bring to Techno that purely European Minimal Techno doesn't have?
Ghetto-Tech brings body, humor, and attitude — three things that European Minimal Techno can sometimes be too serious to include. There's a physicality and a directness to Ghetto-Tech that keeps sets from disappearing too far into their own heads. When you're playing hypnotic Minimal Techno for an hour and the crowd starts to look glassy-eyed, a well-placed Ghetto-Tech track with that bouncing, pelvis-forward energy can snap the room back into their bodies without breaking the spell. It also keeps things from getting too austere — Ghetto-Tech has a sense of humor and a street sensibility that I think makes underground electronic music more human and accessible.
What's in your DJ setup, and does it reflect your minimal philosophy?
My setup is deliberately simple — CDJs and a mixer, minimal effects, nothing that creates complexity for its own sake. The temptation in modern DJing is to use every available tool, every effect, every loop, and I think that often works against the music rather than for it. I want the tracks to breathe. I use EQ surgically and I'm very careful with filters, preferring to let the production of the original track do most of the work. When I do reach for an effect it's intentional and rare enough to be noticed. The minimal setup philosophy mirrors the minimal music philosophy — trust what you have, don't add what you don't need.
THE SCENE
How do you see the OC underground scene and what makes it distinct from LA?
Orange County's underground is more intimate than LA's and in some ways more tightly knit because of that. Everyone who's serious about it tends to know everyone else, which creates a real community rather than an anonymous crowd of scene participants. The downside is that OC hasn't always had the venue infrastructure or the booking budgets that LA has, which can make it harder for local artists to build momentum. But I think that's changing — there's real energy and real intent behind what's being built here right now, and the intimacy of the scene means that artists who commit to it get genuine support rather than being lost in a massive urban noise floor.
What advice would you give to new Techno DJs developing their sound in Southern California?
Study the history of the music you're playing before you start playing it publicly. Techno has a real lineage and a real set of values, and audiences in underground Techno spaces can tell immediately whether you've done the homework or whether you're just playing fast dark music because it's having a moment. Specifically with Minimal — spend time learning to listen to what's not there. Train your ears on the spaces between notes, on the way silence functions in a track. That's the skill set that separates someone who can play Minimal well from someone who just throws on minimalist records and hopes for the best. And be patient with yourself and the scene — Techno rewards the long game.
Where do you see Rex Elvis going from here?
I want to keep pushing the Ghetto-Tech and Minimal combination into spaces where it hasn't been heard before. There are rooms across Southern California and beyond that I think would respond to this sound if they were exposed to it — the challenge is always getting the opportunity to introduce it. I'm also working on original productions that are trying to capture that tension between Detroit rawness and European Minimal architecture in a way that's distinctly mine rather than derivative of either tradition. KEEPITIL has been invaluable for connecting artists in the underground who are doing unconventional things, and I'm excited to keep building within that network.
