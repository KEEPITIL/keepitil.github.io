★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

KriStyle

Pure Techno. No Apologies.

Orange County, CA
Techno
KEEPITIL Artist

Some DJs mix genres. KriStyle doesn't. Based in Orange County, this Techno purist has found a devoted audience in a region better known for its pop and EDM crossover events — proof that when the music is right, the audience will follow. Pure Techno, relentless rhythm, and a refusal to apologize for it. Orange County's underground has been waiting for exactly this. KriStyle brings the lineage of Detroit and Berlin to Southern California dance floors and delivers it without dilution, without compromise, and without a single concession to commercial taste. In a county full of big rooms and bigger productions, there's something quietly radical about a DJ who simply trusts the kick drum to do the work.

"Techno works because it never tries to please everyone. It just does what it does."
THE STORY
What is it like being a Techno purist in Orange County's more commercial market?
Honestly, it's a kind of test — and I think that test has made me better. When you're operating in a market that doesn't hand you easy bookings because of genre alignment, you have to earn every room through the quality of the music itself. OC has a reputation for commercial EDM, and that's earned to some extent, but there's always been a pocket of people here who want something harder and deeper. Finding that pocket and building on it has been the entire story of my career here. The commercial market around me has never been a barrier — if anything, it's clarified exactly why I do what I do and who I do it for.
How did you discover Techno and what was your entry point — Detroit or Berlin?
Detroit first, then Berlin — that's the honest answer, and I think it's the right sequence for anyone who wants to understand Techno as a form rather than just a sound. The Detroit origins are foundational: the social context, the Black American working-class roots, the music as a form of futurism coming out of a city that the mainstream had abandoned. You have to understand that history before Berlin makes full sense. When I then encountered the Berlin lineage — the warehouses, the reunification-era cultural explosion, the way Techno became an almost sacred practice in that city — I understood it as an extension rather than a replacement. Both cities are inside every set I play.
Who are the artists that have shaped your Techno philosophy?
Jeff Mills is the standard — there's no one who better demonstrates that Techno at its most rigorous is also Techno at its most emotional. Watching footage of him play, you see someone who is genuinely in a trance, and the crowd follows. Plastikman, meaning Richie Hawtin's alias, showed me the Minimal end of the Techno spectrum — that you could strip away almost everything and have what remains be more powerful for it. Hawtin himself then extended that into a much broader conversation about technology and music that I find endlessly interesting. And Ben Klock represents the Berlin tradition at its finest — relentless, intelligent, always in service of the dance floor rather than the DJ's ego.
How do you structure a pure Techno set from beginning to end?
A pure Techno set has to breathe, which surprises people who think Techno is just ninety minutes of maximum aggression. I usually open with something more hypnotic and rhythmically complex but not yet at full energy — I want the early crowd to lock in before I start pushing the tempo and weight. The middle section is where I build the most — stacking records that add density and momentum, using EQ and effects to create tension between tracks. And then the closing section is about release and resolution: I want the crowd to feel like they've arrived somewhere, not just that the set ended. A great Techno set has an emotional architecture even if nobody in the room can describe it afterward.
THE CRAFT
What is happening in the Techno audience in Southern California right now?
It's genuinely growing, which is exciting for everyone who's been doing this for years without mass support. There's a new generation of listeners who found Techno through streaming and social media and then went looking for the live experience, and what they're finding is a Southern California underground that has been here all along, just not visible to the mainstream. That fresh energy colliding with the people who've been in the scene for a decade or more creates something really good — a community that has both history and momentum, both depth and enthusiasm. I feel like Southern California Techno is having a real moment.
What technical elements define your mixing approach?
Tempo management is everything in Techno. The relationship between BPM and the physical experience of the dance floor is something I've studied obsessively — there are tempos where bodies naturally sync, and when you hit those, the room changes in a way you can see. I also spend a lot of time thinking about texture: Techno records can sound very similar superficially, but there are enormous differences in the texture of the hi-hats, the decay of the kick, the character of any synth lines. Matching those textures across tracks is what makes a Techno mix feel seamless rather than just metronomically accurate.
How do you find new Techno records and what makes a record worth buying?
I'm still buying vinyl where I can, which forces a kind of discipline that digital-only crate digging doesn't require. When you're spending money on a physical object, you think harder about whether a record is actually great or just good. Beyond that, I follow labels more than individual artists — when you find a label whose A&R judgment you trust, you can pre-order releases with confidence. And I listen to a lot of sets by DJs I respect to hear what they're playing before it's widely released. The discovery process is part of the practice, not just the output.
What is your advice to DJs who are chasing trends rather than developing their own sound?
Trends are a treadmill — by the time you've caught the current one, it's already fading, and you're spending all your energy running toward something that will never hold still. The DJs who last are the ones who developed a point of view first and then got good enough at expressing it that the audience came to them. That might take longer. It is definitely less comfortable. But the alternative is spending your entire career being derivative of someone else's taste, and that's not a career — it's an imitation. Go deep into one thing until you know it better than anyone around you. That specificity is the rarest and most valuable thing in a market full of generalists.
THE SCENE
What venues in OC are doing the right thing for underground Techno?
I'll say it's more about promoters than venues — the right promoter can turn almost any space into the right room for Techno. The promoters who are serious about sound quality and who book based on musical fit rather than social media follower count are the ones who build lasting communities. Orange County has a handful of those, and I'm grateful for every one of them. The venues that allow promoters to take risks on unfamiliar music are equally important, and there are more of those than people assume if you know where to look and who to talk to.
How does the OC Techno scene connect with the LA scene?
They're more integrated than the geographical divide suggests. DJs move back and forth constantly, crowds do too, and promoters in both markets know each other well. LA has more infrastructure and more venues with serious sound systems, so there's always a pull northward for bigger opportunities. But OC has something LA sometimes lacks — intimacy. The rooms are smaller and the crowds feel more like communities than audiences. Playing OC Techno nights feels personal in a way that can get lost in LA's scale. I value both contexts and try to exist in both.
What does KEEPITIL represent for where you are in your career?
It represents the acknowledgment that what's happening underground in California is real and worth documenting. There's a version of the story of electronic music in California that only mentions the festivals and the commercial acts, and KEEPITIL is actively writing a different version — one that includes the DJs who've been grinding in smaller rooms, building genuine communities, staying true to the music without waiting for permission from the mainstream. Being part of that counternarrative matters to me. It's the kind of recognition that actually means something because it comes from inside the culture.
