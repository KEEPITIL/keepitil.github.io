★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

Demek

The Mind Has an Underground Too

Los Angeles, CA
Psychedelic Industrial Techno
KEEPITIL Artist

Industrial Techno already pushes the limits of what a body can absorb on a dance floor. Demek adds the psychedelic dimension — textured, layered, disorienting in the best possible sense. Los Angeles-based but operating in a headspace that exists outside geography, Demek creates sets that are as much a mental experience as a physical one. The result is Techno that doesn't just hit — it haunts. LA's warehouse circuit has long supported artists willing to take the audience somewhere extreme, and Demek occupies that space with conviction: sets that treat the dance floor as a consciousness-altering environment, where four-on-the-floor kicks become a carrier signal for something much stranger and much more profound than percussion alone.

"Psychedelic Techno is what happens when the music stops being just music."
THE STORY
How did you arrive at Psychedelic Industrial Techno as a description of your sound?
It wasn't a decision so much as an eventual recognition. I spent years playing Techno that didn't fit neatly into any existing sub-genre label — it wasn't melodic enough for the psytrance crowd, it wasn't stripped enough for the pure Minimal Techno crowd, and it was too textured and disorienting for the straightforward Industrial Techno crowd. I kept describing it to people in approximations — it's Industrial, but with this psychedelic quality, these layered sounds that seem to move around your head — and eventually I just started owning the description. Psychedelic Industrial Techno is what it is, and the specificity of the label is more honest than trying to force it into something it isn't.
What does the LA warehouse and underground rave circuit offer that club spaces don't?
Warehouses offer the most important thing that psychedelic music requires: spatial scale. When you're playing music designed to alter the listener's perception of sound and space, you need actual space — high ceilings, concrete walls, room for sound to move and reflect and create its own geography. A standard club with acoustic treatment is designed to make the sound clear and manageable. A warehouse is designed to store things, and that utilitarian brutality is exactly right for Industrial Techno. The acoustics are raw. The visual environment is industrial by default. Everything in the space is already working in the same aesthetic direction as the music, and that alignment creates experiences that simply cannot happen in a designed room.
Who are the artists that have most influenced your sound?
Ancient Methods is the reference I come back to most — the way that project uses Industrial textures not as ornamentation but as structural material, building Techno tracks that feel genuinely threatening without losing their musicality, is something I aspire to constantly. Surgeon has been making intense, intelligent Techno for over thirty years and his evolution shows that this music can deepen rather than calcify over time. Consumer Recreation Services, the project associated with the duo behind that name, represents the outer edge of where Industrial and Techno meet experimental noise music — that boundary is exactly where I want to work. And Blawan's production approach — the physical rawness, the sense that the sound itself might be dangerous — is something I try to channel in how I sequence and layer tracks in a live context.
How did you find the LA underground rave community?
Through the music first, then through the people. Los Angeles has a larger underground Techno community than its pop-culture reputation suggests, and it's been running for decades. I found my way into it through record stores and online communities that were sharing obscure Industrial and Techno material, then started attending events, then started playing them. The community is genuinely welcoming to people who are serious about the music — there's an immediate recognition between people who have done the listening and the people who haven't, and once you're recognized as someone who has done the work, doors open. LA's underground has a meritocracy to it that I respect enormously.
THE CRAFT
How do psychedelic elements actually enter Techno music — what does that mean concretely?
It happens in several layers simultaneously. At the most basic level, it's about sound design — using synthesis to create tones and textures that behave unexpectedly, that seem to shift in character as they sustain, that create the acoustic illusion of movement or depth that isn't physically present. Above that, it's about rhythm in ways that subvert simple counting — polyrhythmic elements that create competing pulse sensations, that pull the listener's internal timekeeper in multiple directions at once. And at the highest level, it's about set architecture — using the sequencing of tracks to create a cumulative effect on perception, so that an hour into the set, the listener is hearing the music differently than they were at the beginning. That last layer is the most psychedelic thing a DJ can do, and it has nothing to do with any individual track.
How do you build a set that alters perception rather than just providing entertainment?
Repetition and patience — two things that don't come naturally in an entertainment culture addicted to novelty. Perception begins to shift when you've been inside the same sonic world long enough that your brain stops processing it as an external event and starts incorporating it into its own operation. That takes time — usually thirty to forty-five minutes minimum. So the first part of any serious set I play is about establishing a stable field: consistent energy level, consistent texture, consistent rhythmic language, letting the audience acclimatize before I start introducing the elements that will actually displace their baseline. The impatient version of what I do is just loud Techno. The patient version is something else entirely.
What technical tools do you use to achieve psychedelic effects in a live mix?
Effects processing is central — delay, reverb, and pitch manipulation used not decoratively but structurally, to change the spatial character of a track in real time. I also use loop points and cue points in ways that most DJs don't — triggering sections of a track out of sequence, creating rhythmic structures that weren't in the original recording. Beyond that, EQ becomes a sculpting tool: removing and reintroducing frequency ranges in cycles creates a pumping, breathing quality that mimics perceptual fluctuation. The most important tool, though, is the overall sound system. None of the psychedelic elements work if the system can't reproduce them accurately, and substandard sound systems flatten the dimensionality that makes this music what it is.
How do you source music this niche — where does Psychedelic Industrial Techno live?
Mostly in the margins of the internet and in small-run physical releases from labels that don't have marketing budgets. Bandcamp is still the most important platform for niche Techno discovery — the depth of catalog there is extraordinary if you know what you're looking for. Beyond that, I follow a network of DJs and producers who work in adjacent spaces and pay attention to what they're releasing and playing. There are Discogs rabbit holes that can consume days. And I pay close attention to labels — when a label with the right sensibility starts releasing material, I pre-order everything they put out and trust the curatorial judgment. In a genre this niche, the label is often the most reliable signal.
THE SCENE
Who else is doing Psychedelic Industrial Techno in Los Angeles?
There's a small but committed community of artists working in this space in LA, and the connections between them are real. We tend to know each other, support each other's events, and share music before it's publicly released. I won't name names without permission — that's a decision each artist makes for themselves — but I will say that what I find in this community is a seriousness of purpose that is genuinely rare. Nobody is doing this for commercial reasons, because there are no commercial reasons to do it. Everyone here is making and playing this music because they feel some form of compulsion toward it, and that shared compulsion creates a genuine bond. The community is small, but it is not casual.
How does LA's geography and culture shape Psychedelic Industrial Techno specifically?
Los Angeles is a city of surfaces and depths — everything here has a glossy public face and a hidden interior, and that duality is perfect for Industrial Techno, which is itself about the relationship between hard exterior surfaces and inner psychological states. The sprawl of LA also means there are enormous industrial spaces in unexpected corners of the city, warehouse districts that exist in geographical and economic limbo, that become available for exactly the kind of events this music needs. The city's tolerance for the weird — its long tradition of housing artists, mystics, subcultures, and outliers — means that even very niche music can find an audience if it's sincere. LA doesn't always reward sincerity, but it never entirely punishes it either.
What does KEEPITIL represent for underground artists like you who operate outside any mainstream?
KEEPITIL is a documentation project as much as a music platform, and that matters enormously for artists working in spaces that mainstream music media will never reach. The history of underground music is full of scenes and artists who were important in their moment and completely invisible to any official record of what happened — no coverage, no archive, nothing but the memories of the people who were there. KEEPITIL is making a different choice, saying that what happens underground in California is worth documenting while it's happening, not rediscovering twenty years later. For someone doing what I'm doing, being included in that documentation feels like an act of genuine solidarity with the culture rather than a commercial transaction. That's exactly what the underground needs more of.
