★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

RA1N

Temecula's Underground Voice

Temecula, CA
Underground Electronic
KEEPITIL Artist

Temecula is wine country by reputation, but RA1N doesn't make wine country music. Operating out of Riverside County's southern edge, RA1N has become one of the clearest voices in Temecula's growing underground electronic music community. In a city better known for its wineries than its warehouses, RA1N is building something: a base, a community, and a body of work that proves underground music doesn't require a major city address.

"You don't need LA to build a scene. You just need people who care."
THE STORY
What does it mean to build a music scene in Temecula — what are the specific challenges and advantages?
The challenge is visibility — Temecula doesn't have the existing infrastructure that LA or even San Bernardino has developed over decades, no established warehouse circuit, no neighborhood that's historically associated with underground culture. But the advantage is that everything you build here is genuinely new, uncontested, yours in a way that's almost impossible in a city where every space and every night is fighting for position in an established hierarchy. There's also a hunger here — people who grew up in Temecula and found their way to underground electronic music through the internet or through trips to bigger cities and came back wanting what they'd found out there. Building for those people, in their city, without requiring them to drive two hours to have an experience worth having, is the whole project.
How did you get into DJing — what was the path from listener to performer?
It started with the frustration of not hearing what I wanted to hear at the events I was attending. I'd go to shows in Riverside or LA and there would be moments — sometimes whole sets — where I could feel exactly what the room needed and it wasn't what was being played. That frustration is either the thing that makes you stop going to shows or the thing that makes you pick up equipment and figure out how to do it yourself. I chose equipment. The first year was about technical competence: learning to mix, developing an ear for when transitions work and when they don't, building a catalog. The second year was about learning what I actually wanted to say with the music. I'm still learning both, but somewhere in there it became a practice rather than a hobby.
What does underground electronic mean to you — how do you define the genre?
Underground electronic is less a specific sound than a relationship between sound and community — music that prioritizes the experience of the people in the room over the preferences of people outside it, that exists in spaces and at price points and at hours that commercial music doesn't serve. In practice it pulls from techno, from house, from ambient and industrial and every other branch of electronic music that has developed a genuine underground culture. What unifies it is the intention: this music is made for and by a community rather than for a market. That's a disposition that the music itself has to earn and re-earn with every set and every track.
Who are the local underground artists you connect with most closely — who's building this scene with you?
The KEEPITIL community has been the most important connection for me — artists like Aztekan in San Bernardino, the crews in LA and OC, the wider Inland Empire and Riverside County underground. Building a local scene doesn't mean building in isolation; it means rooting yourself somewhere specific while staying connected to the broader community that shares your values. The artists I connect with most are the ones who are doing the same thing in their own cities — not trying to replicate LA, not treating their city as a stepping stone, but genuinely believing that where they are is worth building in and building for. That's the energy that creates real scenes rather than temporary clusters of people waiting to move somewhere else.
THE CRAFT
How do you adapt your sets to different contexts — a Temecula event versus an LA or Riverside show?
The music itself doesn't change dramatically — the tracks I believe in work across different contexts because they're built on genuine quality rather than scene-specific appeal. What changes is the pacing and the arc: a Temecula crowd that's still establishing its relationship with underground electronic needs a slightly more gradual introduction than an LA crowd where you can assume a certain baseline of genre knowledge. Riverside shows tend to run somewhere in between — there's a stronger underground infrastructure there, more experience in the room, but also more heterogeneity in what people expect. The underlying skill is the same in all contexts: read the room in front of you and serve the people who are actually there, not an imaginary crowd you prepared for in advance.
What's your approach to energy management in a set — how do you think about peaks and valleys?
Energy management is the central craft of underground DJing, and the mistake most newer DJs make is confusing energy with tempo or intensity — playing faster or harder is not the same as building energy. Real energy management is about creating the conditions where a peak means something, which requires the valleys that make the peaks perceptible. A set that's at maximum intensity throughout has no peaks — everything is the ceiling, which means nothing is particularly exciting. The sets that land hardest are the ones that give the crowd room to breathe between the moments that demand everything from them. Underground electronic is particularly good at this because the genre conventions allow for extended periods of relative restraint that make the eventual release feel earned rather than arbitrary.
What does community building look like in practice — what are you actually doing beyond playing shows?
Social infrastructure: connecting artists who don't know each other yet, helping organize events where the lineup reflects the full range of what the local scene contains rather than the same rotating cast, supporting people who are earlier in their development by giving them opportunities to play. Documentation also matters — working with platforms like KEEPITIL to create a record of what's happening here, because scenes that aren't documented tend to be invisible outside their immediate geography and that invisibility limits what they can become. The practical work of scene-building is mostly unglamorous: making calls, responding to messages, showing up to events that aren't yours, being a consistent presence in the community over years rather than months.
What's the Riverside County electronic community like compared to what's happening in LA?
Riverside County's underground is more DIY, more community-driven, less subject to the commercial pressures that shape LA's scene because the economic incentives are different at this scale. That creates music and events that feel more like genuine community expression and less like cultural product — which is exactly what the underground is supposed to feel like. The limitation is resources and reach: the infrastructure that LA's decades of underground culture have built doesn't exist here yet, and building it takes time. But the spirit is exactly right, and I'd argue the spirit is the thing that can't be manufactured or imported. The infrastructure can be built. The care that the Riverside County underground community has for its own music is already there.
THE SCENE
What are your goals for Temecula's underground scene in the next few years?
A consistent event series rooted in Temecula — monthly or bi-monthly shows that establish the expectation among the local community that underground electronic music is part of what this city offers, not something you have to leave to experience. Artist development infrastructure: a way for the next generation of Temecula DJs and producers to develop their skills and get their first opportunities to perform in front of an audience. And connection to the broader California underground network — Temecula events that bring artists from San Bernardino and OC and LA to this city, building the kind of cross-pollination that keeps a scene from becoming insular. The goal is a scene that Temecula's community owns and leads, not one that exists here as an outpost of somewhere else.
What does KEEPITIL's platform specifically mean for Temecula and Riverside County artists?
It means being seen as part of California's underground rather than as an isolated footnote to it. Before platforms like KEEPITIL, artists in cities like Temecula were essentially invisible to the wider California electronic community — there was no aggregating context that connected what was happening in Riverside County to what was happening in LA or the Bay. KEEPITIL creates that context, which changes both the perception of the work and the actual opportunities available to artists doing it. When someone searching for underground electronic music in Southern California can find Temecula artists alongside LA artists on the same platform, it validates the scene and attracts people to it who wouldn't have known to look here.
What would you want someone who's never been to a Temecula underground event to know before they come?
That it's real. That what's happening here isn't a simulation of a bigger city's scene or an attempt to replicate what exists elsewhere — it's a genuine community of people who love electronic music and are building spaces for it in their own city with their own hands. The shows might be smaller, the venues less polished, the production more DIY than what you'd find in a major market. But the music is serious and the community is generous and the energy that comes from a room full of people who built the scene they're dancing in is something you can't manufacture at scale. Come with an open mind and you'll find something that LA's underground, for all its resources, can't always offer: a scene that belongs to the people in it.
