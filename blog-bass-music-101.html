Bass Music 101: From Dubstep to Riddim, Hybrid Trap to Future Bass

KEEPITIL Desk June 17, 2026 Music Education · Bass Scene

"Bass music" is a genre label that covers more sonic territory than almost any other in electronic music. It's the reason two people can both say they like bass music and then have completely different reactions when they meet at an event — because one came for melodic dubstep and the other came for 145 BPM riddim that sounds like industrial machinery. This guide maps the territory.

What All Bass Music Has in Common

Despite the range, bass music subgenres share a few consistent characteristics: sub-bass frequencies that you feel in your chest rather than just hear, syncopated rhythms that prioritize percussive impact, and an emphasis on the drop as a structural element. Most bass music tracks follow a tension-and-release architecture — a buildup that creates expectation, and a drop that delivers it, often violently.

The bass itself is usually created through heavy modulation of synthesizers (particularly the use of FM synthesis, wavetable synthesis, and aggressive filtering) rather than sampled instruments. The "wobble" of classic 2012-era dubstep, the grinding distortion of riddim, and the clean, pitched-up synths of future bass are all products of these synthesis techniques applied differently.

The Major Subgenres

Dubstep (Classic / Melodic)
BPM: 138–142
Origin: South London, 2001–2006
Key Labels: Mau5trap, OWSLA, Never Say Die
The foundational bass genre — half-time rhythms (where the kick falls on beat 3 rather than 1 and 3), heavy low-end frequencies, and the "wobble" created by LFO-modulated bass synths. Classic dubstep (Benga, Skream, Digital Mystikz) is dub-influenced and relatively sparse; American dubstep (Skrillex, Excision) pushed the energy and aggression significantly. Melodic dubstep adds major-key synth leads and emotional buildups to the heavy drops.
Riddim
BPM: 140–145
Origin: United States / Online, ~2012–2015
Key Labels: Subsidia, SubCarbon, OneOOne
Riddim strips dubstep down to its most mechanical, repetitive core — simple two-bar bassline patterns that loop with hypnotic persistence, heavy distortion, and minimal melodic content. The sound prioritizes groove and weight over emotional arc. It's aggressive, relentless and deceptively difficult to mix well; the best riddim sets find subtle variations within a narrow sonic range that keep a crowd moving for long periods.
Hybrid Trap / Mid-Tempo
BPM: 70–100 (half-time feel)
Origin: North America, ~2017–2019
Key Artists: Rezz, Born Dirty, MIZE
Hybrid trap sits between hip-hop influenced trap (with its rolling 808s and hi-hat rolls) and dubstep's heavy bass processing. The mid-tempo range (often around 85–95 BPM) creates a heavy, deliberate feel — tracks that walk rather than sprint. Hypnotic, repetitive, and often darker in aesthetic than other bass subgenres. Artists like Rezz helped popularize this end of the spectrum.
Future Bass
BPM: 130–150 (half-time perception)
Origin: North America / Australia, ~2014–2016
Key Artists: Flume, San Holo, What So Not, Ekali
The most melodic corner of the bass music umbrella — future bass uses pitched-up vocal chops, major-key supersaw synths and emotional buildups to create drops that feel like catharsis rather than assault. The "chords" characteristic of future bass (stacked, heavily-processed synth chords that blur together into a single texture) are its most identifiable sonic signature. Lost in Dreams Festival is the dedicated SoCal event for this genre.
Drum & Bass (DnB)
BPM: 160–180+
Origin: London / UK, ~1992–1994
Key Labels: Hospital, Metalheadz, RAM Records
The fastest and most UK-rooted of the bass genres — drum & bass is built on Amen break-influenced drum programming at high tempos, with sub-bass that sits beneath complex percussion. The scene has a distinct culture compared to American bass music: deeper ties to UK grime, jungle, and garage. Liquid DnB is melodic and jazz-influenced; Neurofunk is harder and more technical; Jump-Up is energetic and crowd-oriented.

What's Currently Happening in SoCal

The Southern California bass music scene in 2026 is dominated by Bassrush events (Insomniac's bass arm), several independent promoters in the OC/IE corridor, and a growing number of underground nights at smaller venues in the Arts Districts of both LA and Santa Ana. The crossover between bass music and LA's emerging "midnite" club scene — late-night events with lower-BPM, darker aesthetics — is producing some of the most interesting sounds currently coming out of the region.
