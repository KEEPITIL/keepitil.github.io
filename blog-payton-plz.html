★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

Payton Plz

San Francisco's House Is Bouncing

San Francisco, CA
Bounce / Techno / Jackin House
KEEPITIL Artist

San Francisco has always had a relationship with underground music that other cities can only envy. Payton Plz operates in that tradition — but with a bounce, a jack, and a Techno undertow that makes the Bay Area's classic house roots feel entirely modern. The blend of Bounce energy, Techno drive, and Jackin House groove is exactly the kind of synthesis that SF's dance floors have always demanded and always rewarded. In a city where the underground music scene has survived economic upheaval, displacement, and the slow erosion of venue space, Payton Plz represents something enduring: the conviction that San Francisco will always be a house city at heart, no matter what happens to it on the surface.

"Jackin House is just the feeling of a room that can't stop moving. I'm trying to bottle that."
THE STORY
What was it like growing up in and around the SF Bay Area house scene?
San Francisco has an incredible, specific house music history that you absorb almost by osmosis if you're paying attention. The city's connection to disco and funk, the influence of LGBTQ+ culture on the development of house here, the Wicked crew and the outdoor raves of the nineties — all of that is in the air in San Francisco in a way it isn't anywhere else. Growing up around that gave me a sense of house music as something with genuine roots and genuine stakes, not just a genre but a community practice. That's the foundation everything else I do is built on, even when I'm playing Bounce or Techno material that doesn't sound obviously connected to those roots.
How did you discover Jackin House and Bounce specifically?
They came to me through different channels that eventually converged. Jackin House I found through Chicago — the DJ Sneak and Derrick Carter lineage, the rougher, more percussive side of house that Chicago was developing in parallel to the smoother New York sound. Bounce I found partly through New Orleans traditions and partly through UK Bounce, which has its own identity. What connected them for me was energy — both are music that demands physical response, both prioritize movement over reflection, both have a directness that I find completely honest. When I started playing them together, I realized they were speaking the same language in different accents.
How does San Francisco's underground differ from LA's in feel and practice?
San Francisco is smaller and denser, which means the community is more tight-knit and the shared history is more present in the room. When you play a SF underground event, there's often someone in the crowd who has been going to underground events in that city for twenty years, who knows where this music came from and what it cost to keep it alive. That creates a different kind of listening — more historically aware, more likely to recognize a reference, more invested in the cultural lineage of what they're hearing. LA has energy and scale that SF can't match, but SF has depth and memory that LA sometimes lacks. I value both, but SF is home.
Who are the artists who have had the most impact on your sound?
Frankie Knuckles is the foundation of everything — the Godfather of House didn't earn that title by accident. His ability to make a room feel safe enough to be completely themselves while simultaneously losing themselves in music is something I try to study every time I listen to his recordings. Larry Heard, meaning Mr. Fingers, showed me the emotional depth that house music could reach — that this was not surface music, that it could carry real weight. Derrick Carter is the reason I take Jackin House seriously as a distinct form with its own intelligence. And DJ Sneak, who is criminally underappreciated, is the person who showed me how to be rough and musical simultaneously.
THE CRAFT
What does the current resurgence of house music mean to you?
It's complicated, because every "resurgence" brings both genuine new energy and a lot of surface-level engagement that misses the point. There are people discovering house music right now who are going deep — studying the history, finding the original records, understanding the cultural context — and those people are genuinely adding to the community. And then there are people who have latched onto an aesthetic without the substance, who know the look of house music without knowing its soul. My job is to DJ for the former and educate the latter, not by lecturing but by playing music that shows the difference between something real and something borrowed.
What does Techno add to a set that is primarily Jackin House and Bounce?
Structure and momentum. Jackin House and Bounce are inherently social — they pull people toward each other, toward the floor, toward a kind of collective joy that is specifically warm. Techno is more inward, more hypnotic, more about individual experience within a collective context. When I bring Techno into a Jackin House set, I'm adding a spine to the warmth — giving the energy somewhere to go that feels inevitable rather than scattered. The contrast makes both genres stronger. After a run of Techno, when the Jackin House hits, the room responds to it with more feeling than if the Jackin House had been playing all night without that contrast.
How do you read a SF crowd specifically?
SF crowds tend to be more physically expressive than they sometimes get credit for — the stereotype of the intellectual, cerebral Bay Area audience is real but incomplete. When you give a SF crowd something they love, they respond physically and completely. The challenge is getting there, because SF crowds are also more skeptical than some. They've seen a lot of DJs, they know what good looks like, and they won't give you the benefit of the doubt just because you're behind the decks. You have to earn it. Once you've earned it, though, a San Francisco crowd will follow you anywhere — they're the most loyal audience I've ever played for.
What is your technical approach to mixing across three different sub-genres?
Tempo management is the first tool. Bounce, Jackin House, and Techno can all exist in overlapping BPM ranges, which gives me room to transition between them without jarring the crowd. I also pay close attention to key — playing harmonically compatible tracks in sequence makes genre transitions feel musical rather than arbitrary. And I think about energy level as a separate dimension from genre: sometimes I use a Techno track at the same energy level as the Jackin House I just played, so the transition feels smooth horizontally even as it shifts the texture vertically. When it works, the genre doesn't announce itself — the feeling does.
THE SCENE
What is the state of underground house music in San Francisco in 2025?
It's surviving and in some places thriving, which given what SF has been through — the tech boom displacement, the venue closures, the pandemic years — is genuinely remarkable. The community has proven more resilient than the real estate market gave it credit for. There are new promoters who have found creative solutions to the venue problem: pop-up events, outdoor parties, unusual spaces that aren't traditional clubs. And the commitment level of the people who stayed through the hard years is extraordinary — they kept the culture alive when it would have been easy to let it go. San Francisco underground house in 2025 feels like something that survived for a reason.
What venues or events in SF are doing it right?
I'm cautious about calling out specific names because the landscape shifts so quickly, and a venue that's doing it right today may not have the same booking policy in six months. What I look for and support is consistent programming that prioritizes music quality over celebrity bookings, sound investment that shows the promoter cares about how music actually sounds rather than just how loud it is, and community-building events that create a sense of belonging for regular attendees. The specific venues matter less than those principles. Wherever those principles are being practiced, the SF underground is being held.
What does KEEPITIL mean to the Bay Area underground?
KEEPITIL represents something important: the acknowledgment that California's underground is not a single thing, that it exists in multiple cities and multiple scenes simultaneously, each with its own character and history. By including Bay Area artists alongside Southern California artists, KEEPITIL is documenting the diversity of what California underground actually is — which is messier and more complex and more interesting than any single narrative about it. For the SF community, being included in a California-wide conversation feels validating in a way that purely local recognition doesn't. It says: what you're doing here matters beyond the city limits.
