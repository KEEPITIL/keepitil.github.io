★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

TMZEE

Orange County Raw

Orange, CA
Underground Electronic
KEEPITIL Artist

Orange, California is the kind of city that gets overlooked — wedged between Anaheim, Santa Ana, and Fullerton, without a single landmark that puts it on the national map. TMZEE doesn't need a landmark. Operating in Orange County's underground circuit with an unpolished, raw energy that fits the city's no-frills character, TMZEE has carved out a reputation as one of OC's most authentic underground voices. There's no pretense in the music and no calculated image management in the presentation — just genuine sound made by someone who cares about music more than they care about how they appear to care about music. In a scene where authenticity gets performed almost as often as it's actually lived, TMZEE is the real thing: a DJ whose commitment to unfiltered, honest underground sound comes through in every set they play. Orange County's underground has a gem in this city that nobody talks about, and TMZEE is the proof.

"Raw is not a flaw. Raw is where music comes from before it gets cleaned up."
THE STORY
Tell us about the underground scene in Orange specifically — what's it like?
Orange is one of those cities that nobody really talks about when they talk about OC's music scene, which honestly works in our favor. There's no pressure to perform for an outside audience or to make things look a certain way for people who are watching from somewhere else — it's just the local community doing what they do. The underground scene here is small and genuine, built around people who actually live in and around Orange and who found each other through the music rather than through any coordinated effort. That kind of organic connection is harder to build in cities where the scene is more visible, because the visibility itself changes the dynamic of who shows up and why.
What does "raw and unfiltered" mean in the context of your music?
Raw means I'm not making decisions based on what sounds polished or what would fit comfortably in a playlist — I'm making them based on what feels honest and what creates the reaction I want in a room. Unfiltered means I'm not editing out the edges of my taste to appeal to a broader audience; if something is rough or challenging or doesn't follow the obvious path, I'll still play it if it's right for the moment. There's a whole industry of people whose job is to make underground music more palatable for mainstream consumption, and I'm not interested in that pipeline. The rawness is where the feeling lives — once you start sanding it down, you lose the thing that made it interesting.
How did you get into DJing in the first place?
It started with the music itself — I was deep into listening and collecting before I ever thought seriously about DJing. The transition came from going to events and watching how a good DJ transforms a room, and wanting to understand how that worked mechanically and artistically. Once I started playing around with mixing, it became clear pretty quickly that this was how I wanted to engage with music rather than just being an audience for other people's sets. The early period was a lot of practicing in private and figuring out what my instincts were, which takes time because at first you're just imitating what you've heard rather than developing your own approach.
How do you connect with other OC underground artists, and what does that community look like?
Most of the real connections in OC's underground happen through repeated presence at the same events over time — you see the same people at enough things, you start talking, and eventually you're collaborating or supporting each other's sets. Social media helps, but the actual relationships are built in person at events. OC's underground community is genuinely tight-knit in a way that I think is specific to scenes that haven't been overrun by outside attention — people are there because they want to be, and that shared commitment creates real bonds. Artists like those on the KEEPITIL roster are part of the same network, and having that collective identity helps everyone have a larger footprint than they would individually.
THE CRAFT
What has KEEPITIL meant for your exposure and visibility as an artist?
KEEPITIL has given me a way to be findable that's rooted in the actual scene rather than in generic social media churn. When someone discovers KEEPITIL and starts exploring the roster, they're getting a curated window into what OC's underground actually is — not an algorithm's guess at what underground music should be, but the real thing as represented by the people who are actually doing it here. For an artist based in a city as overlooked as Orange, that kind of contextual visibility matters enormously. It's the difference between being found because of who you are and where you're from, versus just floating in an undifferentiated sea of content hoping the right person happens across you.
Describe your approach to building a set — how do you think about the arc of a performance?
I think about sets as having an emotional logic that doesn't always follow the obvious BPM or energy escalation pattern. Sometimes the most powerful moment in a set is a drop in intensity at an unexpected point, because it recalibrates what the crowd is feeling and makes the next surge hit harder. The raw quality in my music comes through in how I sequence things — I'm not trying to create a smooth, commercially coherent journey, I'm trying to create something that feels like it has real stakes and real feeling. That sometimes means making choices that wouldn't test well with a focus group but that feel absolutely right in the specific room with the specific people who are there.
What influences are most evident in your sound?
The underground electronic music that moves me most tends to have a certain grit to it — music that doesn't hide its process, where you can hear the decisions and the energy that went into making it. Beyond pure electronic influences, I'm drawn to the rawness that comes out of punk and industrial music, that sense that something is at stake in how the music is made and delivered. Those qualities don't belong to any one genre and they translate into how I approach DJing — I want my sets to feel like something that matters is happening, not like a professional service being rendered smoothly and without friction.
What are your goals for the next chapter of your career?
I want to keep building my presence in OC's underground while reaching more people beyond the immediate local circuit. The goal isn't mainstream recognition — it's finding the pockets of underground culture across Southern California and beyond where what I do resonates with people who have similar values about music. I'm also thinking about producing original material that captures the same rawness I bring to my sets, because I think there's a gap in the recorded music landscape for the kind of sound I'm working with. Whatever happens next, it needs to stay honest — the moment I start making decisions based on what someone else thinks I should sound like is the moment I lose the thing that makes this worth doing.
THE SCENE
What makes Orange County's underground scene distinct from the LA underground?
OC's underground is more intimate by necessity — we don't have the venue infrastructure or the population density that LA has, so the scene stays smaller and tighter. That intimacy creates a different kind of culture: you actually know the people you're playing for, and they know you, which changes the dynamic of a performance in ways that are hard to replicate in a larger, more anonymous setting. LA has incredible energy and a massive diversity of scenes, but it's also a place where the underground can get swallowed by the sheer volume of things happening and the constant flow of people moving through. OC's underground has a permanence to it — the core community is local and invested in building something over time rather than chasing whatever is momentarily hot.
What does a successful underground event look like to you?
A successful underground event is one where the energy in the room feels genuinely collective — where everyone present has contributed something to the atmosphere rather than just consuming it. It's the events where people are fully present, not checking their phones constantly or performing their attendance for social media, but actually there in the room with the music and with each other. Those events feel rare and precious when they happen, and they're what remind you why underground culture matters as something separate from commercial music entertainment. The rawness I bring to my sets is specifically designed to create those moments — to pull people into the present and make them feel something they can't just passively receive.
What do you want people to take away from a TMZEE set?
I want people to leave feeling like they experienced something real — not performed or packaged or calculated, but genuinely felt. I want them to be slightly surprised by what they heard, because surprise means something got past their defenses and reached them at a level that expected music can't. If someone walks out of one of my sets and says "that was weird in the best possible way," that's the ideal response. Underground music at its best gives people an experience that's unavailable anywhere else, and that's the standard I'm trying to meet — something that could only happen in this specific underground context, with these specific people, on this specific night.
