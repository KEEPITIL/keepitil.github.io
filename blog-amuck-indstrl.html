★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

AMUCK INDSTRL

Infrastructure as Instrument

Los Angeles, CA
Industrial / Hard Techno
KEEPITIL Artist

Industrial music has always been the sound of systems under pressure — machines running past their design limits, structures grinding against each other. Amuck Indstrl takes that lineage and brings it to the dance floor via Hard Techno, creating sets where the industrial heritage of electronic music's experimental past collides with the physical demands of the modern underground rave. Los Angeles is the city. The warehouse is the instrument.

"Industrial isn't ugly. It's just honest about what making something requires."
THE STORY
What Industrial music lineage are you drawing from — where does this start for you?
Throbbing Gristle is the unavoidable beginning — Genesis P-Orridge and Cosey Fanni Tutti's insistence that anything could be music, that sound produced by industrial processes deserved the same attention as sound produced by trained musicians, that discomfort was not a failure of craft but a choice. Einstürzende Neubauten took that further, literally building instruments from construction equipment, from metal scrap, from the infrastructure of a city being rebuilt after wartime destruction. Test Dept brought it into the political — using industrial rhythm as protest, as the sound of labor against capital. That tradition of using machine sound to make emotional and political statements about the world machines have built is what everything I do descends from.
How does Industrial become dance music in your hands — what's the bridge?
The body has always been part of Industrial music even when the music wasn't explicitly designed for dancing — the physical impact of metal percussion, the way distorted low frequencies create a pressure in the chest that registers before the mind interprets it as music. Hard Techno provides the structural framework that makes that physical impact repeatable and navigable: the four-four kick, the tempo, the arrangement conventions that give a body something to lock onto. Within that structure, I layer textures and sounds from the industrial tradition — noise, found recordings of mechanical processes, distorted synthesis that references machine noise rather than conventional musical sound. The result is music that can function as dance music while carrying all the weight and intention of the industrial tradition it comes from.
What's LA warehouse culture like from the inside — what does it provide that club rooms don't?
Freedom is the primary thing. In a warehouse, the sound system can be louder, the programming can be stranger, the expectations of the crowd are different because they've made a choice to seek something out rather than walk into a space defined by a door policy and a bottle service menu. Warehouse shows attract people who are genuinely curious rather than people who are there because the venue is fashionable — and genuine curiosity makes a completely different kind of audience. The aesthetic context of an actual warehouse — the industrial space itself, the exposed infrastructure, the physical evidence that this was a place where things were built — also connects directly to the music I'm making. The venue is an argument for the music.
Who are the contemporary artists in this space that you're watching closely?
Phase Fatale for the way his productions sit at the exact intersection of the industrial tradition and contemporary techno without compromising either — his records sound like they were made in a factory that was also a club, which is exactly where I want to be. Orphx from Canada, who have maintained a continuously interesting and evolving practice at the industrial-techno intersection for over two decades now. Vatican Shadow — Dominick Fernow's project — for the way it uses military and political imagery as a conceptual frame for industrial techno that's genuinely dark without being nihilistic. These artists demonstrate that the industrial lineage and club functionality are not in opposition.
THE CRAFT
What do you want people to feel in your sets — not just physically, but emotionally?
I want people to feel the weight of the world they live in and also the possibility of moving through it. Industrial music at its best doesn't just describe the machine — it describes what it means to be a human being inside a machine-made world, all the alienation and power and beauty and horror that entails. On the dance floor, that can become something strange and transformative: you receive the full weight of that reality through the music, and you respond to it not with paralysis but with movement. There's something politically meaningful about that, about taking the sound of industrial systems and using your body to refuse its dominance while existing inside it. That's what I want a crowd to feel.
How do you select and arrange tracks in an industrial Hard Techno set — how do you think about flow?
Industrial sets have a different relationship to dynamics than most techno. The genre can sustain a very high average intensity for longer periods than most music — the crowd that comes to an industrial set has calibrated their expectations accordingly — but that doesn't mean there's no dynamic architecture. I think about textural variation more than energy variation: a sequence of tracks that are all maximal in terms of tempo and kick impact but vary in their texture and noise content creates its own kind of movement. A brutally minimal kick-and-noise track followed by something with more harmonic complexity feels like a shift even if the tempo and energy haven't changed. Texture is the melody of industrial music.
How do you find new music in a genre that's not well served by mainstream platforms?
Bandcamp is essential — it's where independent industrial and hard techno labels operate with the most autonomy and where catalog depth is most accessible. I follow a network of artists and curators whose taste I trust, particularly in Europe where the industrial-techno intersection has maintained a more consistent presence over the decades. Physical media still matters in this genre — there are industrial releases that exist only on vinyl or cassette, where the format is part of the artistic statement, and you don't find those on streaming platforms. Record fairs, direct contact with labels, and the kind of sustained engagement with a community that makes it possible to hear about releases before they're publicly announced — these are the research methods.
What does Hard Techno provide that pure experimental Industrial doesn't?
A body to come back to. Pure experimental industrial — Merzbow, early Whitehouse, the most extreme noise music — makes demands on the listener that include the option to disengage entirely, to receive it intellectually or conceptually without any physical participation. Hard Techno insists on the body through the kick drum and the tempo. It says: regardless of what your mind is doing with this information, your body is already in it. That physical insistence changes the relationship between listener and sound from contemplative to participatory, and participation changes everything about how the industrial content is received. The hardest ideas land differently when your body is already saying yes.
THE SCENE
How does the KEEPITIL underground connect artists who might otherwise operate in isolation?
Before platforms like KEEPITIL, underground artists in different parts of California existed in near-total isolation from each other — the LA scene didn't necessarily know what was happening in the Inland Empire, the Bay Area, or San Diego, and the scene in each of those places didn't necessarily know about each other. KEEPITIL creates a shared context, a California underground identity that makes it possible to see the connections between what Amuck Indstrl is doing in LA warehouses and what artists like Aztekan are building in San Bernardino. That shared context is the beginning of a real scene rather than a collection of isolated micro-communities.
What do you think LA brings to industrial and hard techno that other cities can't?
Scale and contradiction. Los Angeles is the largest urban economy in the western United States and also a city with profound inequality, enormous creative energy and entrenched commercial pressure, a history of political radicalism and a deeply embedded entertainment industry. Industrial music has always been made in response to the conditions of industrial capitalism, and LA is one of the most extreme expressions of those conditions available in the contemporary United States. The music makes sense here in a way that's rooted in the specific reality of what this city is. That doesn't make LA industrial better than Berlin industrial or London industrial — it makes it specifically and irreducibly Angeleno.
What's your vision for what you're building — where does this go?
Original production that carries the full lineage of industrial music into contemporary club contexts. More collaboration with visual artists who work with industrial aesthetics — projection, installation, performance — because industrial music has always understood that sound alone is not the complete experience. And more connection between the California underground communities: playing shows that bring LA warehouse culture into contact with the Inland Empire underground, with the South Bay, with the San Gabriel Valley scenes that are developing their own distinct identities. The underground needs circulation to stay alive, and I want to be part of building the circulatory system.
