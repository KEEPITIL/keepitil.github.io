★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

SE PAPID D

Just House. Pure and Simple.

Los Angeles, CA
House
KEEPITIL Artist

Some DJs need to explain their music. se Papid D doesn't. House music — just house, real house, groove-forward and soul-connected house — is the whole conversation. Based in Los Angeles and operating in a city where every electronic sub-genre competes for attention, se Papid D's commitment to pure House music is both a stance and a gift to anyone who's ever needed a room that just felt good.

"House is a feeling first. Everything else is secondary."
THE STORY
How did you get into House music in LA — what was the entry point?
LA's house music scene has an underappreciated history — there were crews and DJs here in the late eighties and nineties who were doing incredible work that most of the city's official music history ignores in favor of rap and rock. I came up in the slipstream of that history, finding records and mixtapes and eventually parties that connected directly to the original Chicago and New York house lineages through the people who carried those sounds west. Once you hear real house music in a good room with the right sound system, the appeal is total and immediate — there's no sub-genre curiosity, no genre tourism, just a direct recognition that this music was made specifically for the experience you're currently having. That recognition is what made me want to be on the other side of it as a DJ.
What does "pure" House mean to you — how is it different from Tech House or Electro House?
Pure House has a soul element that its commercial derivatives have systematically removed because soul is inconvenient for mass appeal — it's too specific, too rooted in particular communities and particular emotional histories. Tech House, at its most commercial, is house music with the feeling replaced by function: technically proficient, dance-floor effective, but emotionally hollow in a way that becomes apparent over the course of a long set. Electro House took house's energy and attached it to EDM's production values and structural logic, which produces something that peaks effectively but doesn't sustain. Pure House — the kind that comes from the original Chicago tradition and its genuine inheritors — has warmth and a sense of community that you can hear in the chord progressions, in the way vocals are treated, in the relationship between the kick and the bass. That warmth is not optional. It's the whole point.
Who are the artists that made you understand what House could be at its best?
Marshall Jefferson wrote "Move Your Body" in 1986 and the house music template has never fundamentally improved on it — the piano, the gospel sense of uplift, the community-building function encoded directly into the arrangement. Frankie Knuckles, the Godfather of House, for showing that a DJ's job is to create a space where people can become themselves rather than perform themselves. Kevin Saunderson's Reese productions for showing how the bass could carry the full weight of a song's emotion without a single word being sung. And Joe Smooth's "Promised Land" for being one of the most spiritually complete house records ever made, a track that connects house music's Chicago roots to the civil rights tradition in a way that makes the genre's full meaning undeniable. These artists are why I do this.
What's the LA House music community like right now — who's keeping it real?
LA's house community is dispersed but deep — there are pockets of genuine commitment to original house values scattered across the city, in areas with strong Latino and Black communities that maintained the culture through the decades when it wasn't commercially fashionable. The underground circuit keeps it alive: smaller venues, semi-private events, outdoor gatherings where the community that cares about house for the right reasons can find each other without the pressure of commercial viability. KEEPITIL's work of connecting and documenting these communities is particularly important for house in LA, because the scene's depth is invisible from the outside while being extremely real from the inside.
THE CRAFT
How do you keep House sets feeling fresh in 2024 when the genre is forty years old?
The catalog is so deep that freshness is never actually the problem — there are extraordinary house records from every decade that most current DJs aren't playing, which makes them completely fresh to a contemporary audience even though they were made in 1988. Beyond that, the freshness in a house set comes from the combination rather than the individual tracks: juxtapositions you've never heard before, a sequence that creates meaning through contrast, a moment where a very old record and a very new record reveal that they're answering each other across forty years of music. The craft of house DJing is curatorial and relational — the tracks are fresh in context even when they're not fresh in isolation. And honestly, the best house records don't age because they're about human feelings rather than sonic trends.
What's your favorite kind of room to play — what's the ideal context for pure House?
Mid-sized, unpretentious, with a sound system that prioritizes warmth over volume. I want a room where people are there to dance rather than to be seen, where the demographics are mixed in a way that reflects house music's original community rather than the homogeneous crowds that commercial versions of the genre attract. Ideally a room that's been dancing for a while before I start, so the space is already warm and the social permission to move has been established. I'm not interested in being the DJ who wakes a room up — I want to be the DJ who takes a room that's already alive and carries it somewhere it couldn't have gotten to on its own. That requires a certain kind of crowd and a certain kind of room, and when those conditions are right, house music does things that no other genre can do.
How do you build a set — are you planning in advance or responding in the moment?
Always in the moment, but from a preparation that's so thorough that the moment has everything it needs. I know my records at a level where I don't have to think about what's coming next — the selection emerges from reading the room and the music makes the decisions rather than my conscious mind. What I prepare in advance is the depth of the catalog: knowing that for any particular mood or energy state the room might be in, I have twenty records that could answer it. The set is improvised; the vocabulary it's improvised in took years to build. That combination of real-time responsiveness and deep preparation is what separates a DJ who can play well from a DJ who can play well under any conditions.
What does groove mean to you — how do you think about it technically and emotionally?
Technically, groove is the relationship between rhythmic elements that creates the sense of forward momentum that makes a body want to move — the specific placement of the kick relative to the bass, the amount of swing in the hi-hat pattern, the way a synth stab lands slightly behind the beat to create tension that the next element resolves. Emotionally, groove is the sensation of time slowing down and becoming hospitable, of the music creating a space where the body's movement feels inevitable rather than chosen. The best house music has both — the technical precision that makes the groove work mechanically and the emotional intelligence that makes it feel like an invitation rather than an instruction. When a groove is truly right, you don't decide to dance. You discover that you already are.
THE SCENE
What does KEEPITIL's platform mean for House music in Southern California?
KEEPITIL gives house music in Southern California a home base — a place where the work being done in OC and the IE and LA and San Diego can be seen together as a connected community rather than as isolated individuals. House music has always been about community first, and a platform that creates community context for the artists doing this work is not a small thing. It's also a documentation project at a moment when documenting underground music is urgently important — the spaces where underground house culture lives are increasingly threatened by gentrification and real estate pressure, and having a record of the music and the artists means that the culture survives even when the specific spaces don't.
What do you want a first-timer at a House event to understand before they walk in?
That house music is communal music — it's not a performance you watch, it's an experience you participate in, and your participation changes the music as much as the DJ's selection does. The energy a crowd puts into a house set comes back through the DJ's choices, creating a feedback loop that makes a good house night genuinely collaborative rather than just a one-way broadcast. Walk in without expectations about which specific records will be played or what the energy will be, and be willing to let the music take you somewhere rather than insisting on knowing the destination in advance. House music's deepest gifts are not predictable, and the people who receive them are usually the ones who came in with open hands.
Where do you see yourself going as an artist — what's the next chapter?
More original music — producing tracks that contribute to the house catalog rather than only drawing from it, making music that might be someone else's foundational record the way the classics were mine. More long-form sets: the four or five hour set that can explore the full range of house music's history and emotional territory is a form I want to develop, because the single-hour DJ slot, however well-executed, doesn't have the space to do what house music at its best requires. And more connection with the broader KEEPITIL community — playing alongside artists from different genres, finding the connections between house and techno and Industrial and the other sounds in California's underground, because those connections make all the music richer.
