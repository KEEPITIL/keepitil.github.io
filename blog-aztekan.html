★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

AZTEKAN

Ancient Frequencies, Modern Dance Floors

San Bernardino, CA
Hypnotic / Deep Techno
KEEPITIL Artist

San Bernardino's underground music scene doesn't get the press of LA or SF, but Aztekan doesn't need the press. Operating in the realm of Hypnotic and Deep Techno — where repetition is a feature, not a bug, and where rhythm becomes ritualistic before it becomes recreational — Aztekan builds sets that operate on a frequency that bypasses the conscious mind. The Inland Empire underground has felt it. The rest of California is catching on.

"Hypnotic Techno doesn't entertain you. It takes you somewhere."
THE STORY
What does Hypnotic Techno mean to you, and why does it work on people the way it does?
Hypnotic Techno is about accessing something deeper than enjoyment — it's about the brain's response to repetition, to subtle variation over long cycles, to the way a groove can settle into your nervous system and rewire your sense of time. The genius of the genre is that it's not trying to excite you in the traditional sense. It's trying to replace your ordinary state of consciousness with something more open, more present, more surrendered to the moment. When it works, the room disappears and all that exists is the rhythm and your body responding to it. That's not entertainment. That's closer to meditation — or ritual.
How does the Inland Empire underground compare to what's happening in LA?
The Inland Empire underground is its own thing. It's not trying to be LA, and that's what makes it special — there's a rawness here, a lack of industry pressure, a freedom from the need to perform coolness for an audience that's half there to be seen. The people who come out to shows in San Bernardino, in Riverside, in the IE broadly, are there for the music and nothing else. That creates a different kind of energy in the room, a different relationship between the DJ and the crowd. LA has incredible energy, but the IE has intention, and in the underground, intention matters more.
What artists shaped your direction — who put you on to the hypnotic path?
Blawan was a revelation — the way his productions use industrial rhythm as a kind of physical force, the way repetition becomes weapon rather than wallpaper. Shackleton showed me how ancient rhythmic concepts and modern electronic production could coexist, how dubwise bass and hypnotic loops could be sacred and forward-looking at the same time. Ancient Methods taught me about maximalism within minimalism — how a small number of elements, pushed to their limits, can create overwhelming complexity. And Plastikman — Richie Hawtin at his most reduced — showed me what happens when you strip everything away and trust the groove to carry the full weight of an hour-long journey.
San Bernardino isn't the obvious base for underground electronic music. Why stay?
Because San Bernardino needs it more, and that means it matters more. The city has a complicated story — economic pressure, history, a cultural richness that doesn't always get the recognition it deserves — and electronic music, underground music especially, has always been the sound of communities that the mainstream overlooked. There's something right about building hypnotic, ritualistic techno in a city that has had to develop its own resilience, its own rhythm, its own way of surviving and celebrating simultaneously. San Bernardino made this music make sense to me before I even understood the genre.
THE CRAFT
How does repetition become transcendence in a long DJ set?
It's about the relationship between what's repeating and what's changing — the best hypnotic sets have a fixed structural element, a loop or groove or rhythmic anchor, that the listener locks onto and trusts, while everything else shifts in micro-increments that register below conscious awareness. You're not noticing that something changed; you're noticing that the room feels different, that your body has moved without your mind approving the movement. Over the course of a two or three hour set, those micro-changes accumulate into a complete journey — you arrived somewhere specific by moving through changes you barely registered in real time. That's the craft of hypnotic techno: making the journey invisible while the destination is completely felt.
How do you structure a long set in this genre — how do you think about time?
Time in a hypnotic set is not linear the way it is in everyday life or even in most club music. I think about sets in phases rather than tracks — there's a phase of entry, where the groove establishes itself and the room starts to surrender, a phase of depth, where the most reduced and meditative material lives, and a phase of emergence, where the energy slowly returns and the room comes back to itself. The transitions between phases happen over much longer timescales than most DJs work with. A single element might fade in over ten minutes. A kick drum might disappear for fifteen. It's close to how you'd score a film rather than how you'd program a radio show.
What does your record selection look like — are you digging for specific sounds?
I'm always looking for music that has what I call "trance potential" — tracks that can function as anchors, that have a quality of groundedness that allows them to loop and evolve without losing the listener's trust. A lot of contemporary techno is exciting on the first listen but doesn't sustain attention at the deeper levels that hypnotic sets require. I'm looking for recordings where the groove is the composition, where the texture has enough internal complexity that it rewards extended attention. A lot of that comes from specific labels — Prologue, Hessle Audio, Subsist — but also from older material that the current generation of producers built on.
What's the future of Deep and Hypnotic Techno in California?
I think California's underground is moving toward a deeper engagement with slower, more hypnotic sounds as a direct response to the saturation of Hard Techno and the relentless tempo escalation of the last few years. There's a hunger for space, for room to breathe within the music, for sets that don't peak every eight minutes. The Inland Empire underground is part of that shift — we've never been as invested in the faster, harder sounds that dominate streaming and festival lineups. I think what's been happening here for the last few years is going to look prescient in about eighteen months when the broader California scene catches up to where the underground has been.
THE SCENE
What does KEEPITIL mean to the Inland Empire underground community?
KEEPITIL is the connective tissue that the IE underground has needed — a platform that documents, promotes, and takes seriously the work being done outside of LA's spotlight. For artists operating in San Bernardino, Riverside, or anywhere in the Inland Empire, having a home base like KEEPITIL changes the equation. It says: your work is worth archiving, worth presenting, worth showing to the wider California electronic community. The underground needs infrastructure, and KEEPITIL is building it here.
What does a great night look like for you as a DJ — what are you going home happy about?
The best nights are the ones where you can feel the room shift into a collective state — where individual bodies in the space stop being separate people making individual decisions and become one organism responding to sound together. That sounds abstract but you can feel it happen, usually about forty-five minutes into a well-built set, when the fidgeting stops and the noise from conversations drops and everyone is just in it. Those nights, you go home knowing the music did what it's supposed to do. The social function of hypnotic techno fulfilled itself. That's everything.
What would you tell someone who hasn't experienced hypnotic techno live yet?
Give it more time than you think you need. The first twenty minutes of a hypnotic set can feel monotonous if you're comparing it to the immediate gratification of more commercial electronic music — the drops, the builds, the peaks every four minutes. If you stay through that initial adjustment period, something starts to happen that's genuinely different from any other musical experience available in a club context. Your relationship to time changes. Your body takes over. What felt repetitive starts to feel inevitable, like it's the only possible music for this moment. That's when you understand why we do this.
