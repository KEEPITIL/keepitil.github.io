From a South Central Warehouse to a Global Empire: The History of Insomniac & EDC

How a 17-year-old handing out flyers in Los Angeles turned a 300-person warehouse party into Electric Daisy Carnival — and the biggest name in American rave culture.

KKEEPITIL Editorial ·August 1, 2026·History·8 min read

Every giant festival you've ever bought a ticket to owes something to a kid in Los Angeles who couldn't sleep, couldn't stop throwing parties, and refused to believe the warehouse scene had a ceiling.

Today, Electric Daisy Carnival fills the Las Vegas Motor Speedway with hundreds of thousands of people a night and beams the same neon dream to México, Europe, and Asia. But the whole empire traces back to one person, one city, and a run of illegal-feeling warehouse parties that most people at the time assumed would never leave the underground. This is the origin story of Insomniac and EDC — sourced, and closer to KEEPITIL's own DNA than any corporate history usually admits.

A 17-year-old with a flyer stack

Before Insomniac was a company, it was Pasquale Rotella — a teenager immersed in the early-'90s Los Angeles underground dance scene. According to Insomniac's own account, Rotella threw his first event at age 17, a party called "Unity Groove," featuring DJs Steve Loria and Sean Perry. He wasn't a promoter with a team; he was a kid who had found the thing he couldn't stop doing.

The obsession had a name soon enough. In October 1993, Rotella officially launched Insomniac with its first underground event — a party in a warehouse in South Central Los Angeles. The scale is the part people forget: those early shows drew about 300 people, moved to a new warehouse venue nearly every week, and were run almost entirely by one person. Rotella scouted the locations, curated the décor, booked the talent, and handed out the flyers himself. If that sounds familiar to anyone who's thrown a DIY show, it should — it's the exact grind KEEPITIL is built around, just before the internet existed to help.

Those early shows drew about 300 people and moved to a new warehouse nearly every week — run almost entirely by one person.

How "Electric Daisy Carnival" got its name

The EDC name has a more tangled history than the branding suggests. The phrase "Electric Daisy Carnival" didn't originate with Rotella — Philip Blaine, another figure in the early rave scene, had used it first, and in 1995 granted Rotella the right to use the name. The two then co-produced the legendary Organic '96 event before the EDC banner made its official debut the following year.

That debut came in 1997, when Electric Daisy Carnival launched at the Shrine Expo Hall in Los Angeles. It was still a Los Angeles event, still rooted in the warehouse-and-expo-hall world Rotella came up in — but it now had a name that could carry weight, a name people would eventually tattoo on themselves.

The move that changed everything: Las Vegas

For over a decade EDC grew as a Southern California institution, but the leap that turned it into a national phenomenon was geographic. In 2011, Insomniac moved Electric Daisy Carnival to Las Vegas, staging it across two nights in late June. Vegas gave EDC something Los Angeles increasingly couldn't: room to become enormous — a speedway-sized canvas, a 24-hour city built for it, and a destination fans would fly in from around the world to reach.

From there the growth compounded. EDC became an internationally acclaimed live-music experience drawing millions of attendees across editions worldwide, and Insomniac expanded into a portfolio of festivals and brands far beyond that first warehouse. In 2013, Live Nation acquired a majority stake in Insomniac — the underground had, by any measure, gone very much overground.

Why this history matters to the underground

It's easy to look at EDC now — the fireworks, the corporate ownership, the resale prices — and file it under "not underground anymore." Rotella himself has pushed back on that framing, arguing in interviews that EDC keeps underground values in a lot of ways even at massive scale. Whatever you make of that debate, the lineage is undeniable: the biggest dance-music brand in America started as a weekly, one-man, 300-capacity warehouse party in South Central L.A.

That's the part worth holding onto, because it's the whole thesis of a platform like KEEPITIL. Every massive thing in this culture started small, local, and unglamorous — a flyer, a warehouse, a few hundred people who showed up because someone they trusted told them to. The infrastructure changes; the origin never does. The next Pasquale is throwing a 300-person party in a warehouse right now, and the only real question is whether the scene has better tools to help them than a stack of flyers.

The quick version

Pasquale Rotella threw his first party ("Unity Groove") at 17, founded Insomniac in October 1993 with a 300-person warehouse show in South Central L.A., was granted the "Electric Daisy Carnival" name by Philip Blaine in 1995, debuted EDC at L.A.'s Shrine Expo Hall in 1997, and moved it to Las Vegas in 2011 — where a warehouse party became a global festival empire.
