★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

DSA

Orange County's Bass Frequency

Orange, CA
Dubstep
KEEPITIL Artist

Orange, California might be a small city, but DSA makes a large noise. A Dubstep specialist operating in Orange County's underground circuit, DSA has built a reputation for sets that treat sub-bass not as a background element but as the main character. The .wav is in the name. The frequency is in everything else. While Orange County's entertainment landscape skews toward the polished and the commercial, DSA has carved out space for something rawer and more physical — Dubstep at the weight it was always meant to carry, delivered through sound systems that were built for exactly this purpose, to rooms full of people who came specifically for the impact.

"When the sub hits right, the entire room becomes one person."
THE STORY
How did you get your start in the OC Dubstep scene?
I found Dubstep through internet rabbit holes before I found it through any live scene — downloading mixes, following SoundCloud pages, trying to track down the records that were in the sets I loved. When I started going to shows, I realized there was a genuine underground in Orange County that wasn't easy to find if you weren't already looking, but once you found it, it had everything you needed. My first sets were at small warehouse events and house parties, places where the sound system was the most important thing in the room and everything else was secondary. That taught me to focus entirely on what the music was doing to the space.
What makes Dubstep distinct from other bass genres?
The wobble and the half-time feel — those are the signatures, but the real distinction is the space. Dubstep breathes differently from other bass genres. The tempo is typically slower, which means there's more room inside each bar for tension to build and release, for the bass to move through shapes that faster genres don't have time for. That spaciousness is actually where the emotional power comes from. Drum and Bass hits fast and continuous. Dubstep hits at intervals, which means each hit carries more anticipation and more weight. When you understand how Dubstep uses time, you understand why it sounds the way it does.
How do you engineer your sets specifically for sub-bass impact?
It starts before I arrive at the venue. I spend time understanding the sound system I'll be playing on — its sub capabilities, how it handles different frequency ranges, where it's strong and where it's compensating for weaknesses. Then I select and sequence tracks based on that knowledge. There are records that are incredible on a system with proper sub response and completely ordinary on a system that can't handle low frequencies. I try never to play records past their frequency ceiling. Beyond that, I manage levels carefully throughout the set to keep the sub from compressing — a compressed sub loses all its impact, and that's the one thing I'm never willing to sacrifice.
What does the OC underground Dubstep community look like day to day?
It's small and it's real, which is the best combination you can hope for. The people who are in it are genuinely committed — they're not here because Dubstep is trendy, they're here because this music does something to them that no other music does. There's a mutual respect among the DJs that makes it collaborative rather than competitive, and the promoters who run OC underground events are true believers who do it for the culture rather than the money. When you're in a community like that, even the small shows feel significant, because everyone in the room is there for the right reasons.
THE CRAFT
Who are your primary influences and what specifically did you take from each?
12th Planet is the reason I take Dubstep seriously as a genre with genuine artistic depth — he was one of the first people to prove that American Dubstep could have the same seriousness and craft as its UK origins. Downlink showed me how aggressive and precise Dubstep can be simultaneously — there's nothing sloppy about a Downlink set, every choice is architectural. Excision gave me the template for how a Dubstep show can be a genuine spectacle without losing its underground soul. And Datsik's early era, before his later controversies, produced some of the most creative sound design in Dubstep history and taught me to pay attention to the individual sounds inside a track rather than just the track as a whole.
How does production work feed back into your DJing?
Producing Dubstep teaches you to listen to music differently than pure DJing does. When you understand how a track is built — the layers, the automation, the side-chain relationships — you hear other producers' tracks with a level of analytical depth that helps you make better mixing decisions. I can hear where a track is going before it gets there, which means I can time my transitions to land in exactly the right place structurally, not just rhythmically. Production also keeps me honest — it's harder to be lazy about sound quality when you know how much work went into creating it.
What does your typical track selection process look like before a show?
I spend more time on preparation than most people would guess from watching me play. Before a significant show, I'll spend several hours listening through my library specifically for that context — what venue, what crowd, what time slot, what's happening before and after me. I build loose groupings of tracks that I think will work together rather than a strict set list, because a strict set list takes away the ability to respond to what the room is actually doing. The preparation gives me options. The set is the moment when I choose between them based on what's in front of me.
What equipment defines your technical approach to DJing?
CDJs and a mixer I know intimately — I'd rather play on equipment I've worked with for years than have a more advanced setup I haven't fully internalized. The tools should be transparent, meaning they should disappear and let the music come through rather than drawing attention to themselves. Beyond the playback gear, I'm very attentive to gain staging — keeping levels correct through the whole signal chain from my controller to the house system is the kind of technical discipline that shows in the mix quality even if the audience can't name what they're hearing. Good gain staging is the difference between a mix that sounds professional and one that just sounds loud.
THE SCENE
Where do you see OC underground bass music going?
I see it building critical mass in a way that hasn't happened before. There are more DJs in OC who are taking bass music seriously as a practice, more promoters willing to program it, and a growing audience that knows enough to demand quality. The next phase, I think, is infrastructure — better regular venues, more established monthly events that give the community a consistent gathering point rather than one-off nights scattered through the calendar. Once you have that kind of regularity, the community deepens in ways that sporadic events never allow. I'm working toward being part of building that.
How has Orange as a city shaped your identity as an artist?
Orange is a city that most people from outside OC can't place on a map, and there's something freeing about that invisibility. You're not representing a brand — nobody has a preset idea of what a DJ from Orange sounds like — so you get to define it yourself. There's also a working-class practicality to Orange that I carry into my work: less concern with image, more concern with doing the job right. The city doesn't ask you to perform belonging the way hipper places sometimes do. You just show up and do the work, and that ethos has shaped everything about how I approach DJing.
What does being part of KEEPITIL mean for where you are in your journey?
It means being seen for what I actually am rather than what's convenient to market. KEEPITIL is documenting the California underground in a way that is specific and honest, and being included in that documentation feels like a form of validation that I didn't know I was waiting for. Not commercial validation — underground validation, which is the kind that actually counts. When the community you belong to recognizes your work, that recognition becomes part of the work itself. It gives you energy to keep going, to keep building, to keep making the noise that a small city in OC apparently needed someone to make.
