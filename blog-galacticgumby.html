★ KEEPITIL PRESENTS

GalacticGumby

UK Sounds, California Soil

San Jacinto, CA
Speed Garage / UKG / House / Hard Techno
KEEPITIL Artist

Speed Garage and UK Garage aren't often what you expect to hear at a California rave. GalacticGumby makes that expectation irrelevant. From San Jacinto in Riverside County, this DJ brings the bouncy, chopped vocals and sub-heavy swing of UKG into rooms that have never heard it before — and won't forget it after. The connection between UK underground and California underground turns out to be tighter than anyone thought. Both scenes are built on raw community energy, creative independence, and a total disregard for mainstream commercial music. GalacticGumby is the bridge that makes those two worlds shake hands on the dance floor.

"Speed Garage is a feeling. Once it clicks, you can't unhear it."
THE STORY
How did you discover UK Garage and Speed Garage from Riverside County, California?
The internet made it possible to fall down rabbit holes that geographic location couldn't stop, and that's exactly what happened with me and UKG. I found a mix somewhere online — I honestly can't even remember the exact track or artist that started it — and the sound of those chopped vocals over that swinging 2-step rhythm was completely unlike anything I was hearing in California at the time. I started obsessively hunting down more: MJ Cole, Artful Dodger, DJ Q, all the classic stuff, then the harder Speed Garage tracks. Riverside County doesn't exactly have a UKG infrastructure so a lot of my early education happened through YouTube rabbit holes, DJ sets on SoundCloud, and sheer relentless research.
What are the Speed Garage and UKG artists that made the biggest impact on your approach?
MJ Cole's production work was foundational — tracks like "Crazy Love" showed me how sophisticated and emotionally resonant the genre could be when it was at its best. Artful Dodger basically defined the commercial peak of UK Garage and understanding their craft taught me a lot about how to make a room feel something. DJ Q is more recent but his approach to blending UKG with harder sounds was directly influential on how I started thinking about connecting UKG to Hard Techno. Todd Edwards is the hidden genius of the whole tradition — his chopped-up vocal technique is genuinely unlike anything else in electronic music and studying it changed how I think about sampling and texture.
What's it like building a niche with UK Garage in California — is there an audience for it?
The audience exists, it just doesn't know it yet in a lot of cases. Every time I've played UKG or Speed Garage tracks in a California set, the reaction from people who've never heard it before is almost always the same — immediate, physical recognition that something is right about this music even though they can't place it. The 2-step rhythm activates the body in a way that crosses cultural context; you don't need to have grown up in South London to feel it. Building the niche has required patience and a lot of educational DJing — playing the music in contexts where people can discover it — but the foundations are there and the community around it is growing.
How does the Riverside County underground scene factor into your development as an artist?
The Inland Empire and Riverside County have a raw, unpretentious underground scene that I think is actually perfect for developing an unconventional artistic approach. There's less cultural gatekeeping than you might find in LA or even OC — if the music is good and you can hold a room, people respond, regardless of whether what you're playing fits a recognized genre category. That openness gave me the freedom to develop my UKG focus without feeling like I had to justify it constantly. The IE underground has its own character and its own history, and being part of that community while doing something as niche as Speed Garage has taught me a lot about how to communicate with an audience through music rather than genre labels.
THE CRAFT
How do you connect UK Garage and Speed Garage to Hard Techno in a set? That seems like a significant bridge to build.
The bridge is actually more natural than it looks from the outside. Both Speed Garage and Hard Techno share a core attribute: they're both built around low-end dominance and rhythmic insistence. Speed Garage's sub-bass is physically heavy in a way that's not that different from Hard Techno's kick-bass relationship, even though the styles and tempos differ considerably. What I do in my sets is use the transitional space carefully — often finding Speed Garage tracks that are running toward the harder end of the genre's BPM range, then bridging to Hard Techno tracks that have some of that groove in their rhythmic structure. The crowd often doesn't notice the genre shift so much as they notice the energy shift, which is the intended experience.
What does Hard Techno add to a set that's primarily built around UKG?
Hard Techno adds a kind of industrial gravity that UKG doesn't have — a weight and relentlessness that can take a set to a place of peak physical intensity that even the hardest Speed Garage track can't quite reach. The combination creates a set with a very wide dynamic range: you can be in the swinging, vocal-chopped, almost joyful world of UK Garage one moment, and then the Hard Techno comes in and the room shifts into a completely different register of experience. That contrast makes both genres feel more powerful than they might in isolation. The UKG sections feel lighter and more euphoric by contrast, and the Hard Techno sections feel heavier and more aggressive. Both gain from the relationship.
What does your record digging process look like when you're hunting for UKG and Speed Garage tracks?
It's a combination of Discogs deep dives into the UK garage catalog from the late '90s and early 2000s, hunting through digital platforms for contemporary producers who are keeping the tradition alive, and following the community of UK-based DJs and collectors who are keeping the knowledge of the genre active. There's a generational handoff happening right now with UKG where the original generation of producers is being rediscovered by younger artists who are making new Speed Garage that's informed by both the original era and contemporary production techniques. Finding the connective tissue between vintage Speed Garage and what's being made now is one of the most exciting parts of the research.
THE SCENE
What's the state of the Riverside County underground right now?
Riverside County's underground is genuinely underrated and I think it's about to have a real moment. The IE has always produced creative talent in music and art, but the infrastructure to showcase that talent locally has historically lagged behind. What I'm seeing now is a generation of artists and organizers who are committed to building that infrastructure — regular events, consistent venues, a growing community of dedicated attendees. San Jacinto itself is a smaller city but it's connected to a wider network of IE underground culture that includes Riverside, Moreno Valley, Hemet, and beyond. The scene is real, it's growing, and it's doing it on its own terms.
What's your advice to other artists in the underground trying to build something around a niche sound?
Trust the music first and worry about the audience second. If what you're playing is genuinely great and you believe in it deeply, that conviction translates to a live room in ways that are hard to fake. I've never had to apologize for playing Speed Garage in a set that wasn't expecting it — the music speaks for itself when it's played with skill and intention. But you have to do the work of really understanding your niche before you try to evangelize it. Superficial knowledge of a genre is obvious immediately to anyone who actually knows it, so go deep before you go public. The community you build around a niche sound will be smaller but much more committed than a broad audience built on whatever's trending.
Where does GalacticGumby go from here?
More sets, more cities, and more original productions that try to capture the transatlantic connection I'm exploring with UKG and California underground culture. I want to start documenting the Speed Garage and UKG community in the US more intentionally — there are artists doing interesting things with these sounds across the country and they largely don't know each other exist yet. Building those connections feels like part of the mission alongside the DJing and production. KEEPITIL has been a great starting point for that network-building, and I'm excited to keep expanding it.
