The Track That Named Techno's Soul: "Strings of Life"

How a broke kid from Detroit made a piano-and-strings record with no bassline that became techno's emotional core — and the anthem of a generation of ravers.

KEEPITIL

There's no bassline. That's the first thing that should tell you how strange it is that this became one of the most important dance records ever made.

"Strings of Life" shouldn't have worked. It's a piano riff, a stabbing string arrangement, and a shuffling drum-machine groove with almost no low end — a record that ignores nearly every rule of what makes a floor move, and instead moves something higher up. It did it so completely that Derrick May gave the feeling a name that stuck to an entire genre: hi-tech soul. Here's the real history of how it got made, sourced.

Detroit, 1987

The track was released in 1987 under the alias Rhythim Is Rhythim — Derrick May working with pianist Michael James — and it came out on Transmat, the label May had just founded. May was one third of the Belleville Three, alongside Kevin Saunderson and Juan Atkins: three kids from the Detroit suburbs whose obsession with drum machines, synthesizers, and the late-night broadcasts of radio DJ the Electrifying Mojo laid the entire foundation of techno.

Context matters here. This was Detroit in the middle of its automotive collapse — a city hollowing out — and out of that specific bleakness came young Black artists building the future on secondhand machines. "Strings of Life" is often read as the sound of finding transcendence in a place that had every reason to feel like it had none.

Derrick May gave the feeling a name that stuck to an entire genre: hi-tech soul.

The sound: strings borrowed from an orchestra

The famous string stabs weren't a preset — the track built its arrangement around string sounds sampled, per multiple accounts, from the Detroit Symphony Orchestra, chopped and sequenced into that heraldic, almost baroque fanfare. Combined with a salsa-informed piano groove, the result feels simultaneously refined and ecstatic. Where a lot of early electronic music leaned on hypnotic minimalism, "Strings of Life" did the opposite: it layered, it swelled, it told a story. That dynamic, melodic humanity is exactly what set it apart and made "hi-tech soul" the right description.

How it got its name — and its legend

The title didn't even come from May. According to the track's own history, Frankie Knuckles — the Chicago DJ widely credited as the godfather of house — heard it and suggested the name "Strings of Life." One genre's founding father naming the other's defining record is about as neat a summary of house and techno's shared DNA as you'll ever get.

From there it detonated. "Strings of Life" became one of the signature anthems of the UK's first rave explosion, turning a quiet Detroit producer into a reluctant superstar DJ across the Atlantic. It's been sampled repeatedly over the decades — including by jazz legend Herbie Hancock — and it still lands on floors today, nearly forty years later, whenever a DJ wants to remind a room where all of this came from.

The quick version

Released in 1987 as Rhythim Is Rhythim on Derrick May's Transmat label, "Strings of Life" — built on sampled orchestral strings, a salsa-tinged piano groove, and almost no bassline — was named by Frankie Knuckles, became a UK rave anthem, and defined the "hi-tech soul" that separates Detroit techno from everything that copied it.
